Carlos —

I read this overnight. Strongest chapter in the piece so far. Detailed comments below. Bracketed marginalia in the document itself. Holistic thoughts here:

**What's working:**

The chronology is now legible. A reader who has not been reading this material for eighteen months can follow what happened, in what order, and why each step mattered. This was the central problem with Draft 1. You have solved it. The "phase one, phase two, phase three" structure is austere but it works.

Andrew Lacher emerges as a specific person without your having to give him interior life. The choice not to render his psychology is, I think, the right one for this piece. The reader knows what he did. The reader can infer what they like about who he is.

The line about the original families receiving $14.3 million — landing in the third-to-last paragraph rather than at the top of the chapter — does exactly what you said it would do. It is the line a reader will carry out of the chapter. The previous draft buried it in the second paragraph; the rewrite is much stronger.

**What's not working:**

You are still using "I have called him fourteen times" as a beat. It appears here, and in Chapter 1, and in Chapter 5 (in different language). Pick one place. Cut the others. The repetition reads as the writer reminding the reader of his persistence rather than as a fact about the reporting.

The Helen Fung paragraph in Section 3 — the one beginning "Helen Fung, the attorney who structured the transactions" — is doing two things at once. It is introducing her as a character and it is foreshadowing the moral content of Chapter 5. The introduction is fine. The foreshadowing weakens Chapter 5 when the reader gets there. Cut the second sentence. Let Chapter 5 do its own work.

The section on the Phoenix law firm's relationship to Verdant Holdings is structurally floating. It is sitting between the description of the LLC structure and the description of the consolidation request. I would either move it earlier (before the LLC structure, as part of establishing the cast of actors) or later (after the consolidation, as part of establishing how the consolidation was processed). I do not have a strong view about which. I do have a strong view that it should not stay where it is.

The closing scene — the implicit comparison between the families' $14.3 million and the consolidated portfolio's $200-$400 million — is the right closing scene. But it ends too quickly. You have set up the comparison and then you have stopped before the reader has had time to sit with it. Give it one more paragraph. Not an editorializing paragraph. A paragraph that lets the silence sit.

**A larger note:**

The chapter, as it stands, is the chapter of the piece that is most likely to attract a legal response from Verdant Holdings or its representatives. Not because anything in it is inaccurate — your documentation is meticulous. Because the chapter is the chapter that most directly characterizes the firm's strategy as having been deliberately designed to evade scrutiny. The characterization is, in my reading, supported by the evidence. The characterization is also, in any conceivable legal defense the firm might mount, the central point of attack.

I want you to read every paragraph of this chapter with the question: would I be willing to defend this paragraph, in court, against a hostile cross-examination? Where the answer is yes, leave the paragraph. Where the answer is "probably yes, but I am uncertain," sharpen the paragraph until the answer is unambiguous yes. Where the answer is no, cut the paragraph.

You have written what may be the most legally consequential investigative chapter of your career. I trust your judgment. I am asking you to extend that trust to yourself.

— R
