# Chapter 1 — The Lake That Wasn't

**Draft:** 4 (substantially rewritten 2026-05-14)
**Word count:** 2,840
**Status:** With Reema for first editor read

---

In the photographs from the 1960s, Lake Talavera is improbable. A blue oval gleaming in the southwestern desert near the New Mexico–Texas line, surrounded by a thin halo of cottonwoods. There are picnic tables in the older shots, and a small dock, and a sign that reads *Welcome to Lake Talavera — Anglers' Paradise.* The fish are mostly bass. A man in a straw hat smiles into the camera, holding one up by its lower lip. His name was Eulogio Castaneda, and the lake, in 1962, belonged to him.

It does not exist now. If you drive out to the coordinates the U.S. Geological Survey still maintains as Lake Talavera's center point, you will find a depression in the earth maybe three hundred yards across, ringed by skeletal cottonwoods and the white residue of long-evaporated water. A few mesquite trees have moved in. There is no sign anymore.

The water did not leave because of drought, though drought was the cover story for nearly forty years. It was diverted, decades ago, through a series of decisions that began as small administrative actions and ended as the largest private appropriation of public water in the region's history. The men and women who orchestrated those decisions are still alive. Some of them are still in office. None of them, until recently, had been required to explain what they did.

This story is about that explanation. It is about a place called the Mesa Verde Irrigation District, which controls roughly eleven percent of the surface water rights in three counties, and about the people who run it. It is about a state water board that signed paperwork without reading it. It is about a private equity firm that bought a series of small ranching operations between 2014 and 2019, and which now controls water rights worth — depending on how you measure them — somewhere between two hundred and four hundred million dollars.

It is also about Eulogio Castaneda, who died in 2017 without ever knowing what had been done to him.

---

I learned about Lake Talavera in November 2024, in a kitchen in Mesilla, New Mexico, drinking coffee with a woman who had been a state water board member for six years and who had resigned in 2022 after — she said — refusing to sign something she could not in good conscience sign. She is not yet named in this story. She has reasons. We have agreed, for now, that I will call her Esther in these notes, and that her real name will appear, if at all, only after she has decided the cost is acceptable.

Esther told me about Lake Talavera as if it were the easiest example. There were, she said, harder ones. Larger ones. Ones that touched tribal water rights, which were politically unspeakable. But Lake Talavera was the one she had begun her career trying to understand and had ended her career, she felt, by failing to save.

"What I want you to write," she said, looking at the table and not at me, "is not a story about water. There are a hundred stories about water. I want you to write a story about how a small lake in the desert became a way to make rich people richer, and nobody in any of the offices that were supposed to stop it ever lifted a finger. Or — I shouldn't say nobody. I want you to write a story about why I lifted a finger and it didn't matter."
