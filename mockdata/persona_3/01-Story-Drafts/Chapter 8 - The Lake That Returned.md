# Chapter 8 — The Lake That Returned

**Draft:** 1
**Word count:** 1,780
**Status:** Closing chapter; will be the last written

---

In April 2026, six months into my reporting and three weeks after I had spent two days drinking bad coffee with Helen Fung in Tucson, I drove with Hortensia Sandoval out to the depression in the desert where Lake Talavera had been.

It was a Saturday. Her daughter Yolanda drove us — Hortensia's eyes are not what they were — and we took a cooler with sandwiches and a thermos of horchata that Hortensia had made the night before. We did not speak much in the truck. We listened to a radio station out of Albuquerque that played ranchera music from the 1970s and a brief weather report that promised a dry afternoon, ninety-one degrees, with winds from the southwest.

When we got to the lake, there was, of course, no lake. There was a depression in the earth, ringed by skeletal cottonwoods that had not been alive for thirty years but had not yet fallen down. The wind moved the dry branches. The light was the color of old paper.

Hortensia stood at the edge of the depression for a long time. She had brought a single rose with her. I had not noticed it in the truck. She walked down to the center of where the water had been and she set the rose on the cracked clay floor.

"My grandfather brought my grandmother here," she said. "Before they were married. He told her he wanted to show her a lake in the desert. She did not believe him."

She did not say what she did next, because what she did next was nothing. She stood for another long minute. Then she turned and walked back to the truck, and Yolanda and I followed her, and we drove back to Belen in mostly silence, with the radio off this time.

I do not know what Hortensia was thinking. I did not ask. There are questions a reporter does not ask, not because the answer is unimportant but because the answer is not for the reporter.

---

What I want to write at the end of this piece, and have not yet figured out how to write, is something about the relationship between paper and water.

The water, in the Mesa Verde Irrigation District, is administered through certificates. The certificates are pieces of paper. The pieces of paper, over the past decade, have been moved from one set of hands to another, through legal mechanisms that were, in Helen Fung's careful phrase, lawful at every step. The water itself — the actual water, the molecules that fall as rain and flow through canals — has, for the most part, continued to flow more or less where it always flowed. What has changed is who, on paper, is entitled to it.

This is the kind of distinction that, in a sufficiently sophisticated system, becomes invisible. The paper stops corresponding to the water. The water stops corresponding to the paper. Each becomes its own thing. And eventually — in 2014, in 2019, in the moment when forty-seven families discovered that they had given away something they had not known they were giving away — the paper wins.

There is a way to read this story as a story about water. There is a way to read it as a story about real estate. There is a way to read it as a story about private equity. I have tried, in this piece, to write it as a story about paper, and about the people who knew how to read it and the people who did not.

---

In the days after I drove with Hortensia to the depression where Lake Talavera had been, I called Helen Fung. I told her about the trip. I told her about the rose.

She was quiet on the phone for a long time.

Then she said: "When you write that scene, Carlos, do not editorialize. Let it be what it was."

I said I would try.

She said, "And give Mrs. Sandoval my regards. I do not know her. I will not contact her. But I want you to tell her, when the piece comes out, that she has been thought about."

I said I would.

I do not, yet, know how I will tell her.

**Note from CM, 2026-05-28:**

This chapter is the chapter I have been writing in my head since I started the piece. It is also the chapter I am most afraid will be cut. Reema has not yet read it. I am sending it to her tomorrow. I expect she will say it is too quiet for a closing chapter, and she will be partly right, and we will argue about how much to leave.

What I want this chapter to do is the thing investigative reporting at its best can do, which is to deliver a piece of moral knowledge without insisting on it. The reader has been led, by chapter eight, through fifty thousand words of evidence about a system that took something from people who did not understand what was being taken. The reader does not need me to insist, in the closing chapter, on what the evidence shows. The reader needs me to stand back.

I have been writing for twenty-one years. The lesson I keep relearning is that the last paragraph of an investigation should not be the loudest paragraph. It should be the paragraph that lets the reader carry the rest of the piece out into their day.

I think Hortensia and the rose are the right last image. I think Helen Fung's phone call is the right last conversation. I think the line *give Mrs. Sandoval my regards* is the line I have been waiting, for six months, to be given.

I did not invent it. Helen said it. I wrote it down in the car after I got off the phone. It was the gift the source gave me. I will honor it.
