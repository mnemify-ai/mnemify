# Chapter 7 — The Tribal Question

**Draft:** 1 (very rough)
**Word count:** 1,420
**Status:** Early; this chapter may be split into two or absorbed elsewhere

---

There is a chapter of this story that does not, in the version of the story that runs in the magazine, get fully told. The reason is not, as one might assume, that the story is too sensitive to publish. The reason is that the people who would have to be the sources for that chapter have decided, after extended conversations with me, that they are not yet ready to be the sources for that chapter, and I have agreed to respect that decision.

What follows, then, is an outline of what I know, and a clear statement of what I am not yet permitted to say.

---

The Mesa Verde Irrigation District operates within a watershed that includes lands belonging to a federally recognized tribal nation. The tribal nation has, under treaty rights dating to the nineteenth century and confirmed by federal court rulings as recent as 2008, senior water-rights claims that exceed, in legal terms, the rights of every irrigation district in the region combined.

In practical terms, these rights have rarely been exercised. The tribal nation has not historically had the financial or institutional capacity to enforce its full claim. It has, instead, allowed the irrigation districts to operate as if the senior claims did not exist, while preserving the legal right to assert those claims should circumstances ever require it.

Verdant Holdings, in 2014, would have known about these tribal claims. So would Helen Fung. So would the state attorneys who reviewed the consolidation paperwork in 2019. None of the public documentation around the Mesa Verde consolidations references the tribal claims.

This is not, by itself, evidence of wrongdoing. It is, however, a question that an investigation into the consolidation has to address. The question is: did the private equity firm structure the consolidations on the assumption that the tribal claims would never be enforced? And if so, what were the conversations that led to that assumption?

---

I have, over the past six months, attempted to interview members of the tribal nation's water resources office. I have been treated with respect and with caution. I have been told, in different language by different officials, that the question of the tribal claims is being studied internally and that public comment is, for the moment, not appropriate.

I have come to understand that this is not a refusal. It is a request for time.

The tribal nation's attorneys have, I believe, begun preparing a federal court filing that will assert the senior claims in a way that, if successful, would invalidate or substantially modify the Verdant Holdings consolidations. The filing is not yet ready. It may not be ready before our publication date. The tribal nation has asked me, through an intermediary, not to publish information about the filing in advance of its completion.

I have agreed.

---

[The chapter ends here, in the published version. There will be a short note acknowledging that the tribal question is the subject of a separate, ongoing inquiry. The reader will understand, I hope, that we have made a deliberate editorial choice. — CM]

**Note from CM, 2026-05-27:**

This is the chapter that is most likely to be discussed internally at the magazine before publication. The decision to withhold what I know about the impending tribal filing is one I have made in conversation with Reema and David. They have backed me. The standard at *The Drift* has always been that we do not publish material that would compromise an ongoing process that we have reason to believe will result in a more just outcome than our publication would. The tribal nation's filing, if successful, would do more than our story can do.

This is the right call. But it is a hard call, because the temptation to write the most complete version of the story is real. I am sitting on what is, by some measures, the most important fact of the entire investigation. I will sit on it.

I have written, in my private notes, a longer version of this chapter that will not be published. It is in a file labeled `tribal-question-long-version.md` in my source-files directory. If circumstances change before publication — for example, if the tribal nation's filing is made public before we go to press — I will adapt.

For now: restraint. The chapter is short. The chapter acknowledges what it does not say. The reader is told, in plain language, that there is more here, and that the reasons for not telling it now are the right reasons.

Beatriz, when I described this decision, said: *"This is the part of your job that I respect the most. The deciding what not to say."*

I think she meant it. I want to think she meant it.
