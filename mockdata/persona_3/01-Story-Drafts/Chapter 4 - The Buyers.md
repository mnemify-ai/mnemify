# Chapter 4 — The Buyers

**Draft:** 2
**Word count:** 2,990
**Status:** In progress; awaiting one more interview before completion

---

The man who designed the strategy is named Andrew Lacher. He is fifty-eight years old, lives in Greenwich, Connecticut, and has a law degree from the University of Virginia and an MBA from Wharton. He is, by the standards of his industry, well-regarded but not famous. He sits on the boards of three philanthropic organizations, two of which are devoted to environmental causes. His wife is a former public defender. He has two adult children. He does not return calls.

I have called him fourteen times since January 2025. I have written six letters. I have made one appearance, in March 2025, at the lobby of the building in Greenwich where his firm maintains offices, where I was politely but firmly directed by a receptionist to leave my card and depart. I have spoken to three people who used to work for him, all of whom would speak only on background, none of whom would name him as the architect of the Mesa Verde acquisitions, and all of whom acknowledged, in different language, that he had been the architect.

This is the standard texture of investigative reporting on people who have lawyers and time. They do not come to you. You wait. You build the story without them, and you keep the lines of communication open in case they decide, late and probably too late, to participate.

---

What I know about Lacher's plan comes from several sources. The first is documentary: filings with the Delaware Secretary of State, property records in three New Mexico counties, water-rights certificate transfer records (which became available, partially, after Esther's resignation prompted an internal audit), and a series of internal memos that I obtained through a former employee whose identity I will not publish.

The second is human: the three former employees, Esther's notes, an attorney in Phoenix who handled some of the paperwork and who broke confidence after retiring (I will return to this attorney in a later chapter), a federal agency staffer who was peripherally involved in a related review process, and two members of ranching families who sold land to Verdant Holdings between 2014 and 2017 and who, in retrospect, believe they were misled about what they were selling.

The third is technical: a hydrologist at New Mexico State University who has been mapping the actual water flows in the region for two decades and whose data, when overlaid with the legal water-rights records, produces a picture of a system in which the paper rights have come substantially detached from the physical reality.

---

The plan, as I understand it, had three phases.

**Phase one** ran from 2014 to 2017. Verdant Holdings, through a chain of nested LLCs, acquired thirty-one parcels of ranchland across the three counties. The parcels averaged 217 acres. The purchase prices, in aggregate, totaled $14.3 million. In every case, the deeds were structured to assign the water-rights certificates to a separate corporate entity — a holding company — which was not registered with the irrigation district itself. The district was not, at the time, required to be notified of such re-assignments.

**Phase two** ran from 2017 to 2019. The holding companies, which by then collectively held water-rights certificates representing 47,400 acre-feet of annual surface-water entitlement, applied to the irrigation district to consolidate the certificates and re-issue them to a single new entity, registered in Delaware and managed by a Phoenix law firm. The district approved the consolidation. The approval was processed by a single staff member, who has since died of natural causes, and who left no written analysis of the consolidation request.

**Phase three** began in 2019 and is ongoing. The consolidated entity has begun, quietly, to lease portions of its water entitlement to a series of agricultural operations in two adjacent states. The leases are structured as multi-decade contracts. The valuations, where they appear in court records (because of an unrelated divorce case in 2023), suggest that the entity's water portfolio is now worth between $200 million and $400 million, depending on the assumptions used.

The original ranching families received, in aggregate, $14.3 million.
