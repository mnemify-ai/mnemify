# Chapter 2 — Esther

**Draft:** 3
**Word count:** 3,120
**Status:** In progress; the most difficult section to write

---

She does not look, when you first meet her, like a person who once made the New Mexico State Water Resources Board uncomfortable. She is seventy-three years old, with silver hair pulled back in a single braid, and she lives in a small adobe house on a street where the children of her neighbors call her *señora* when they pass. She makes coffee in a battered enameled pot and serves it without asking whether you want sugar or milk. The first time I drank it, in November 2024, it was bitter and unsweetened and exactly right.

Her professional history is not unusual on paper. She studied agricultural economics at New Mexico State University in the 1970s, married a man who worked in surveying, raised two daughters, and took a job with the state's water division in 1989. She rose. By the early 2000s she was a senior staff analyst. In 2016 the governor appointed her to the State Water Resources Board, where she served two consecutive three-year terms before resigning in the middle of her third, in March 2022.

The resignation is the public part of the story. The reasons given at the time were "personal." She would say, when I asked her about that in our first meeting, only that this was the truth as far as it went. The personal reason was that she could no longer sleep.

---

What she could no longer sleep about, she said, was a vote she had not yet been asked to cast.

In late 2021 a memo had begun circulating among the board's senior staff regarding what was described as a "consolidation administrative action" — a re-issuance of certain water rights certificates held by the Mesa Verde Irrigation District. The memo was technical. It ran to forty-three pages. Esther read it three times, she told me, before she understood what it was asking the board to do. What it was asking the board to do was to legitimize, retroactively, a series of transfers that had taken place between 2014 and 2019. Transfers that, on Esther's reading of state water law, had been illegal at the time they occurred.

"It wasn't even a difficult question," she said, in our second meeting, in January 2025. "It was an obvious question. The transfers had happened without the required public comment period. They had happened without the formal evaluation that's supposed to happen when surface water rights move between non-contiguous parcels. They had happened without anyone asking whether the downstream users — and there were downstream users — would be affected. None of that had been done. And the memo was asking us to say: do it now, retroactively, and ratify what's already been done."

She did not, then, know who had written the memo. She would find out later. (It was a deputy general counsel who had, between 2017 and 2020, been on the legal team representing the private equity firm in question. He had recused himself from the actual vote, when it eventually came. He had not recused himself from drafting the memo that recommended how to vote.)

---

Esther's first instinct, she told me, was administrative. She asked questions. She asked for the underlying documentation of the transfers themselves. She asked for the public comment files that, according to the memo, had been "informally collected." She asked for the formal evaluations that, according to the memo, had been "satisfactorily reviewed." None of these things, she found, existed.

When I asked her what happened when she pointed this out, she paused for a long time before she answered.

"What happened," she said, "is that nothing happened. I wrote a memo. The memo was not put on the agenda. I wrote another memo. The general counsel met with me and explained that the matter was sensitive and that the board would address it in the appropriate way at the appropriate time. I asked when that would be. He said he could not give me a specific date. I asked who was preventing the agenda. He said no one was preventing the agenda. The agenda was the agenda."

---

[Esther will leave the board in March 2022. The chapter ends with her resignation letter — which I have not yet been able to obtain in its full form; the board's archives have only the cover page. Esther herself does not have a copy. — CM]

**Note from CM, 2026-05-22:**
The hardest writing in this chapter is restraint. There is a temptation, when you have someone like Esther on the record, to render her as a hero. She would hate that. She told me, three times in three different sessions, that what she did was the minimum and she did it badly. The chapter has to honor that view of herself without losing the reader's sense of why this woman is sitting at the center of an investigative piece about water and capital.

I keep coming back to a line from Joan Didion: *We tell ourselves stories in order to live.* Esther is telling herself the story of a small failure. The piece needs to allow that story to coexist with a much larger story about what she actually did, which was to keep a paper trail when nobody else was bothering. Without Esther's notes from her last six months on the board, this investigation does not exist.

She does not yet know I am going to write that sentence. I will tell her before publication.
