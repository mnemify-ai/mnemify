# Chapter 5 — The Phoenix Attorney

**Draft:** 1 (rough)
**Word count:** 1,850
**Status:** Early; need a second sitting with the attorney before I can finish

---

The attorney's name is Helen Fung. She is sixty-six years old, retired in 2023, and lives now in a small Spanish-style house in Tucson with two indoor cats and a garden of native plants that she has been cultivating for thirty years. She practiced law in Phoenix for forty-one years, most of it in commercial real estate, the last fifteen of it in a specialty that did not exist when she started: the transactional architecture of western water rights.

I spent two days with her in February 2026. I drove from Mexico City in three days, slept poorly in a series of motels, and arrived at her front door on a Wednesday morning with the kind of gut-tightness that I associate, now, with sources who have decided to speak but have not yet decided how much.

She opened the door, looked at me for a long moment, and said, "Come in. The coffee is bad. I made it anyway."

---

What Helen Fung wanted to tell me was not, at first, what I had come to hear.

I had come to hear about the Mesa Verde transfers. I had come because two former employees of Verdant Holdings had each named her, separately, as the attorney who had structured the paperwork. I had come because her name appeared on every relevant filing in the Delaware Secretary of State records, and on every transfer document in the three New Mexico counties, between 2014 and 2019. She was, by my reading of the documents, the technical author of the legal structure that had moved $400 million of water rights from the ranching families to Lacher's firm. I had come to ask her how that had felt.

What she wanted to tell me, first, was about her father.

Her father, she said, had come to the United States from Hong Kong in 1957. He had worked, for most of his life, as a draftsman for a small architectural firm in Phoenix. He had not made very much money. He had raised three children, sent them to college, and died in 1988 of a heart attack at the age of sixty-one. Helen, the eldest, had gone to law school because her father had told her, when she was fourteen, that the only profession in the United States that protected the people who practiced it was the law.

She told me this in the first hour of our first conversation. I did not, then, understand why. I understood by the second day.

---

What Helen Fung said, in the second day, was that the legal structure she had built for Lacher's firm had been, in her professional judgment, technically lawful at every step.

She said this slowly, as if testing each word.

"Every transfer was within the existing rules," she said. "Every filing was on time. Every disclosure was made to the agencies that, under the rules as they then existed, were the agencies entitled to disclosure. If you ask me whether what we did was legal, I will tell you that yes, in the narrow sense that the word 'legal' has when it is used by attorneys, it was legal."

I did not ask the next question. She answered it anyway.

"I have spent the last three years," she said, "asking myself whether 'legal' was the question that mattered."

---

[The rest of this chapter needs Helen's reflection on what she has concluded. We are scheduled to speak again in late June. She has told me she will not discuss her conclusion until after we have spoken once more about her father. I do not yet know what about her father she wants to tell me, but I can guess: this is, for her, a meditation on the meaning of professional responsibility in a system that does not require ethical responsibility, and she is using her father — who chose a profession because of its protections — as the moral frame.

I will not push for the rest of the conversation. I will let her give it to me at the pace she has chosen.

**Note from CM, 2026-05-23:**

The Helen chapter is the hardest chapter to write in the entire piece because what she did was, by her own account and by mine, legal. The story I am writing depends on the reader understanding that "legal" and "right" are not the same word. Helen's chapter is the place where that distinction lives.

This means the chapter cannot be journalistic-prosecutorial. It cannot read as accusation. It has to read as an extended conversation between two people — Helen and me — about the meaning of legality, in which neither of us is fully comfortable with the position we hold.

Beatriz, after reading the early draft, said: *"You are writing this chapter as though you are sympathetic to her. Are you?"*

I am. I think she is the most morally serious person I have interviewed for this story. I do not yet know what to do with the fact that the person who built the legal structure that did the damage is also the person who has thought most carefully about what the damage was. This is the central tension of the piece.

Sometimes I think it is the only thing in the piece worth reading.
