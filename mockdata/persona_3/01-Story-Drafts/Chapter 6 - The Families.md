# Chapter 6 — The Families

**Draft:** 1 (rough)
**Word count:** 2,210
**Status:** In progress; need to interview two more families before completion

---

There were forty-seven families whose water-rights certificates passed, between 2014 and 2019, from the Mesa Verde Irrigation District into the holding companies that Verdant Holdings eventually consolidated. I have spoken to nineteen of them. The remaining twenty-eight are, in roughly equal portions, dead, unreachable, or unwilling to be interviewed.

The nineteen I have spoken to fall into three groups.

The first group, comprising six families, sold their water rights knowingly and at what they believed at the time to be fair market value. None of them are angry. Several of them are, to put it precisely, grateful. The money allowed two of these families to send children to college who otherwise could not have gone. It allowed another to pay off medical debts that had accumulated over years. These are not the dramatic stories the piece is structured around, but they are the largest portion of the families I interviewed, and they have to appear in the piece. The reader has to understand that not every transfer was a tragedy.

The second group, comprising eight families, sold the land but believed the water rights were inseparable from the land. They were not told otherwise. In several cases, the language of the sale documents was ambiguous enough that an attorney could later argue either reading. None of these families had attorneys at the time of the sale. The transfers were structured by Helen Fung's office and signed by ranchers who were, in most cases, in their seventies and who trusted that the paperwork said what the buyer's representative had told them it said.

The third group, comprising five families, is the group whose stories the piece is largely going to live in. These are families who, in the years after the sale, came to understand what they had given up, and who tried — through various combinations of letters to state officials, complaints to the irrigation district, hiring attorneys they could not really afford — to recover their water rights. None of them have succeeded. Three of them are still trying.

---

The family I want to write about first is the Sandoval family of Belen, New Mexico.

Hortensia Sandoval is seventy-eight years old. Her husband, Manuel, died in 2021 at the age of eighty. They had four children, two of whom remain in the Belen area. The Sandoval family's water-rights certificate, issued to Manuel's grandfather in 1924, was sold to Verdant Holdings in 2016 for $312,000 along with 184 acres of ranchland on which the family had not actively grazed cattle for twelve years.

Hortensia was not present at the sale. Manuel handled it. He came home, she told me, with a piece of paper that he sat down at the kitchen table and read four times, and then he stood up and said: "I don't think I understood what I just did."

He died before he had recovered what he had signed away. Hortensia has, since 2022, been writing letters. The letters are to state officials, to the irrigation district, to the consolidated entity that now holds the water rights, to the law firm in Phoenix. She writes them by hand. She copies them onto thin paper and keeps the copies in a wooden box in her bedroom. She has, as of our last conversation in March, written four hundred and seven letters.

She does not expect to recover anything. She has told me so directly. "I write the letters," she said, "because I want the record to show that someone was writing them."

**Note from CM, 2026-05-25:**

The Sandoval section is the section I have been most afraid to write. I have been working on it, off and on, for three months. I keep finding reasons to put it down.

I think the reason is that Hortensia trusted me. She showed me Manuel's notes, which had been in a wooden box in her bedroom for five years, and which no one outside her family had read. She gave me copies. She trusted that I would write about her husband fairly.

The fair thing to write is that Manuel, in the months before the sale, had been losing some of his clarity. He had been making errors at the small auto repair shop where he had worked for thirty years. He had been forgetting names. He had not been diagnosed, but Hortensia believes — and her daughter Yolanda agrees — that he was beginning a slow cognitive decline that did not fully manifest for another year.

What this means, in the context of the story, is that the man who signed the papers selling the Sandoval water rights to a Phoenix-based entity representing a Connecticut-based private equity firm was not entirely the man Hortensia had been married to for fifty-one years. He was beginning, already, to be someone else.

To write that fairly is to make a sentence that does not condescend to Manuel, and does not let Verdant Holdings off the hook, and does not turn Hortensia into a character in a Steinbeck novel. I do not yet have the sentence. I will keep trying.

Beatriz, when I described this difficulty, said: "You are not writing about Manuel. You are writing about Hortensia. Let her be the one who decides what to say about her husband."

This is, I think, the answer. I will go back to my notes and find what Hortensia said about Manuel and let those be the words on the page.
