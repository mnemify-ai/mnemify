To understand what was done to Lake Talavera, you need to understand the legal geography of water in the American Southwest, which is not the same as the geographical geography. The river flows on the map, but the rights — the legally adjudicated, individually owned, transferable rights to remove water from the river and put it to use — flow according to a doctrine called prior appropriation, which dates to 1849 and which holds that older claims trump newer ones, regardless of geography, regardless of need, and regardless of consequence.

This is the foundational fact. Water in the Southwest is not a public resource being shared. It is a private asset being administered. The state holds title in theory. In practice, the state has spent a hundred and seventy-five years issuing rights to private parties to remove water from rivers, and those rights have accumulated into a kind of underground commodity market that almost no member of the public has ever seen.

The Mesa Verde Irrigation District is one of seventeen such districts in the three-county area. It was formed in 1924, under a state law that authorized landowners to band together for the purposes of constructing and maintaining shared irrigation infrastructure. The original members were small ranching families. They contributed land, money, and labor to construct canals. In exchange they received water-rights certificates issued by the district — certificates that, for most of the twentieth century, were treated as inextricable from the land on which the original ranching took place.

By 2014, when our story begins, the district had 1,872 members. Many of them were the grandchildren or great-grandchildren of the original founders. Some had sold their ranches and moved away but retained their water-rights certificates as a kind of family inheritance. A few had sold the certificates themselves to neighbors. None of these transfers, until 2014, had been to anyone outside the district itself. The certificates were illiquid. They were a regional currency.

---

In 2014 a Delaware-registered limited liability company called Verdant Holdings LLC began purchasing small parcels of ranchland in three of the counties served by the district. The parcels were not desirable as ranches. They were too small, too dry, too far from highways. What they had, however, were water-rights certificates issued by the Mesa Verde Irrigation District, certificates which — depending on which century of administrative paperwork you consulted — were either inextricable from the land they came with or fungible commodities that could be assigned to other holdings.

Verdant Holdings was a wholly-owned subsidiary of a private equity firm based in Greenwich, Connecticut, whose name I will withhold here pending fact-check completion. The firm specialized, at the time, in agricultural infrastructure. Its principals had developed, over several years, a thesis: that water rights in the western United States were systematically underpriced because they were illiquid and because the administrative agencies responsible for them were under-resourced. The thesis was correct. What was novel was the operational plan to exploit it.
