# On Writing About People Without Their Consent

**Author:** Carlos Morales
**Status:** Private reflection
**Date:** 2026-05-02

## What this is

A reflection that does not belong in the piece and that may not belong in any of my professional files, but that I want to write down because thinking about it has become part of how I write the piece.

I am writing about people who have not consented to be written about. Andrew Lacher has not consented. The two named state officials in the Governor's Office have not consented. Several of the board members who voted for the consolidation have not consented. The general counsel who drafted the memo has not consented.

These people will, after publication, find themselves characterized in print in ways they did not invite. They will find their names in a piece they did not agree to participate in. Some of them will be angry. Some will sue. Most will, after a difficult period, return to their lives and the piece will become, for them, a hard episode in a longer arc.

This is what I do for a living. I have done it for twenty-one years. I am better at it than I was when I started. I am also more uncertain about it.

## The professional answer

The professional answer is that journalism is the public's eye on private power. The people I write about have, in the most relevant sense, acquired their status as subjects of journalistic attention by virtue of what they have done. Mr. Lacher acquired the status by structuring the Mesa Verde transactions. The state officials acquired it by being state officials whose decisions are matters of public concern. The general counsel acquired it by drafting the memo.

This is, in classical liberal terms, the social contract of public life. Those who exercise power must accept that the exercise of power is scrutinized.

The professional answer is correct as far as it goes. It is also, in my experience, an answer that gets thinner over time. The first decade of my career I held it without examination. The second decade I held it while examining it. The third decade — which is to say now — I hold it while remaining aware that the answer is, in some hard-to-pin-down way, a partial answer.

## What the answer leaves out

The answer leaves out the people in the lives of the subjects who did not consent.

Andrew Lacher has a wife. His wife is a former public defender. She has not, by any reading of any reasonable doctrine, done anything that warrants her appearance, even tangentially, in a piece of investigative reporting. She will, after my piece publishes, find herself married to a man whose name is publicly attached to a structure I am about to characterize at length.

The general counsel has a family. The family has not done anything. They will, after publication, find themselves connected by name to a public characterization they did not invite and did not contribute to.

The Sandoval family — and here I include the side of the family that has not been part of the reporting — will find their late patriarch described in ways that they may, depending on their relationship to the matter, find painful. David Sandoval, the estranged son, will read about his father in print. I have not asked David's permission.

These people did not exercise the power that the piece is investigating. They are connected to the people who did, by ties that are not of their making. The piece will, by its existence, alter their lives in small ways that they did not choose.

I do not know what to do with this fact. I write the piece anyway. The piece is, on its merits, in the public interest. The merits do not eliminate the cost.

## What I have done

I have, in writing the piece, tried to:

- Name only the people whose actions are central to the matter
- Avoid characterizing the family members of subjects
- Decline to publish photographs of family members
- Decline to mention spouses by name when their inclusion is not necessary
- Refrain from speculating about the interior lives of subjects who have not spoken with me
- Use the smallest amount of biographical detail consistent with the reader's understanding

These choices are partial protections. They do not undo the underlying fact that the piece exists and that its existence has consequences.

## What Beatriz said

I described this difficulty to Beatriz one evening last month, sitting in the kitchen after Marina had gone to sleep. I told her I had been thinking about the children of the people I was about to write about. She listened. Then she said something that I have been thinking about since.

She said: *"You have always written about people who did not want to be written about. What is different now?"*

I said: I do not know what is different.

She said: *"Maybe nothing is different. Maybe you are just older."*

I said: maybe.

She said: *"Or maybe you are writing this piece because you have, finally, the patience to write it. The patience is what allows you to see the people in the lives of the subjects. The earlier pieces did not see them because the earlier pieces did not have the patience."*

I have been thinking about this since. I do not know whether she is right. I think she may be partly right. The patience that allows the piece to be detailed enough to be accurate is also the patience that allows me to see the costs more clearly.

## What I tell myself

I tell myself: write the piece. Write it as carefully as you can. Make the choices about what to include and what to leave out with the awareness that every inclusion has a cost. Do not pretend the cost does not exist. Do not refuse the work because the cost exists. Do the work and pay the cost.

This is, I think, the best I can do. It is not, by some other standard I have not been able to articulate, enough. It is what I have.

## What I will not do

I will not write a piece that pretends to no moral complexity. The piece, as it stands, acknowledges its own complications. It tells the reader, in several places, that the matter is morally weighted in more than one direction. It does not pretend that the people it describes are simply villains.

I will not, after publication, pretend that I have not done what I have done. If the families of any of the subjects ever ask me, directly, whether I considered the costs to them, I will say yes. I considered. I wrote the piece anyway. The piece's value, on my best reading, exceeds the costs.

If they tell me, in response, that I am wrong about the value or about the costs, I will listen. I will not necessarily change my mind. I will listen.

This is, in the end, the contract I have made with myself. I will write what I believe is in the public interest. I will be accountable for what I write. I will not hide from the costs. I will not invent a moral framework that justifies what I cannot justify on its merits.

The piece is on its merits. The merits, I believe, are sufficient. I am prepared to be told that I am wrong.
