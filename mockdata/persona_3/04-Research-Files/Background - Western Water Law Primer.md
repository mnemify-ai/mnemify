## The prior appropriation doctrine

The legal framework that governs surface water in most western states is called prior appropriation. It dates, in its current form, to the 1840s and 1850s, when miners and farmers in the territory that became California arrived at a system for allocating water that broke from the riparian rights doctrine that had been imported from English common law and that governed (and continues to govern) most of the eastern United States.

The riparian rights doctrine holds that whoever owns land that abuts a watercourse has the right to use water from that watercourse. The rights are tied to the land. They cannot be separated. They are shared, in proportion, among all riparian landowners on the watercourse.

The prior appropriation doctrine holds, instead, that whoever first puts water to beneficial use acquires a right to continue putting that water to that use, in perpetuity, regardless of whether they own land that abuts the watercourse. The rights are tied to use, not to land. They can be separated from land. They can be transferred. They are ranked, in order of seniority, by the date of the original appropriation.

This is the foundational fact. In a prior appropriation state, water is not shared. Water is owned, by some users, to the exclusion of other users, in proportions that depend on when the original users first put the water to beneficial use.

## Why prior appropriation matters for this story

Three things follow from prior appropriation that matter for the Mesa Verde piece.

First, water rights are private property. Not a commons. Not a public good. Private property, ownable by persons or by corporations, transferable like any other property, taxable, mortgageable, financiable.

Second, the value of a water right depends not on the amount of water but on the date of the original appropriation. An 1880 right is worth more than a 1920 right of the same volume, because in a drought year, the 1880 right will be served and the 1920 right will not. The dates are not abstract. They are the basis of valuation.

Third, the administration of prior appropriation depends on a class of public officials — usually called state engineers, state water boards, or state water resources departments — whose job is to maintain the records of who owns what right with what seniority. The administration is, in principle, technical and ministerial. In practice, it is heavily political, because the records determine the distribution of an extremely valuable resource.

## How irrigation districts fit in

Irrigation districts are political subdivisions of the state, created under specific statutes in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. They were originally formed by groups of landowners who pooled resources to build canals and other shared infrastructure. The district owns the infrastructure. The members own water-rights certificates issued by the district.

The certificates, originally, were treated as inseparable from the land for which they were issued. In most western states, this treatment has eroded over the past several decades. The certificates have become increasingly transferable. The transfers are governed by state law and by the bylaws of the individual districts. The bylaws vary.

The Mesa Verde Irrigation District's bylaws, as of 2014, did not explicitly address the question of whether certificates could be transferred to non-resident, non-agricultural entities. The bylaws had been drafted in 1924 and amended only sporadically. The gaps in the bylaws were what Verdant Holdings exploited.

## The role of the state water board

The State Water Resources Board, in New Mexico, is the body that adjudicates disputes between water rights holders and that approves consolidations of multiple rights into single entities. The board has, historically, deferred to the local irrigation districts on most matters that do not involve interdistrict disputes.

What changed, in 2019, is that the Mesa Verde Irrigation District requested the board's affirmative approval of the consolidations Verdant Holdings had executed. The board could have declined. The board approved. The board's approval — the piece argues, with Esther's documentation — was based on incomplete information that the board did not require itself to obtain.

## The tribal claims question

Federal law recognizes the senior water rights of federally recognized tribal nations. The rights derive from treaties, executive orders, and federal court rulings — most notably the 1908 *Winters* decision, which held that when the federal government established reservations, it implicitly reserved the water necessary for the reservations' purposes.

The Winters doctrine has been the basis of substantial litigation. In many western watersheds, tribal water-rights claims are not fully adjudicated. The claims exist, in legal terms, but their exact volumes have not been determined.

In the Mesa Verde watershed, the tribal claims have not been fully adjudicated. They are pending. The eventual adjudication may substantially modify the rights administered by the irrigation district.

This is the most legally significant uncertainty in the entire matter. It is the uncertainty I am being asked, by the tribal nation, not to write about in detail.

## What I learned in writing this document

That the law is not, as it is sometimes characterized, broken. The law is functioning according to its design. The design is the problem. The design was created in the nineteenth century to administer a resource that was assumed to be inexhaustible and that was treated as suitable for private ownership. Neither of those assumptions corresponds to current conditions.

The piece is not, fundamentally, about a violation of the law. It is about the law itself.

This is a hard claim to make in a 50,000-word investigative piece, because the form of investigative journalism is, traditionally, the form of "someone broke a rule." The Mesa Verde story is, in its deepest layer, a story about a rule that was followed and that produced a wrong outcome.

I am still working out how to render this in prose without the reader losing the thread.
