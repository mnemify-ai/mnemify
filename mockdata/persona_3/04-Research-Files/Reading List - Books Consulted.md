# Reading List — Books and Long-Form Sources Consulted

**Author:** Carlos Morales
**Status:** Living list; updated as I read
**Date:** 2026-05-20

## Why this list exists

The piece is rooted in original reporting, but original reporting on water in the American Southwest requires that the reporter have, in their head, the historical and intellectual context of a subject that has been written about for a century and a half. This list is what I have read, or re-read, since beginning this investigation in November 2024.

Some of these works have shaped the piece directly. Others have shaped my understanding of how to write about the subject. A few have been useful as cautionary tales — examples of the way a story about water can become a story about the writer's relationship to the West, which is not the story I am trying to write.

## Foundational works

**Cadillac Desert** by Marc Reisner (1986). Re-read in December 2024. The book that established the contemporary vocabulary of writing about western water. Reisner's voice is everywhere in this genre, including in my own writing whether I want it to be or not. I am, throughout my piece, fighting against the gravitational pull of Reisner's framing — the tendency to write water in the West as morality play. The morality is there. The play is not always the right form.

**Rivers of Empire** by Donald Worster (1985). A more academic, more theoretically rigorous treatment of the same territory. Worster's framing of water as the basis of "hydraulic societies" — societies organized around the management of water — has influenced my chapter on the institutional history of the Mesa Verde district. Worster is, in some ways, the better source for the piece I am actually writing; Reisner is the better source for the piece I would have written if I were younger.

**The Great Thirst** by Norris Hundley (1992, revised 2001). Comprehensive history of California water, which is not my subject but which is the template against which every other western state's water history is read. Useful for understanding the patterns that recur.

## On the doctrine of prior appropriation

**Water and the Law in the West** by George Vranesh (1999). Technical legal text. Not pleasant reading. Necessary.

**The Federal-State Conflict Over Water Rights** by Robert Dunbar (1983). Older but still the clearest treatment of how federal and state interests interact in western water administration. Particularly useful for understanding the tribal claims question.

**Wet Law in a Dry Climate** edited by various authors (2008). Anthology of contemporary writing on prior appropriation and its discontents. Several essays have influenced my framing of how the doctrine has failed to accommodate financialization.

## On the broader landscape

**The Color of Law** by Richard Rothstein (2017). Not about water, but about the broader question of how administrative and legal frameworks systematically produce inequitable outcomes without explicit discriminatory intent. The structural analogy to the Mesa Verde matter is one I have been thinking about.

**Killers of the Flower Moon** by David Grann (2017). A book I have re-read twice in the past year. Grann's restraint, his refusal to editorialize, his willingness to let the documentary record do its own moral work — this is the model. If the Mesa Verde piece achieves a fraction of what Grann achieves in *Flower Moon*, I will consider it the best work of my career.

**The Quiet World** by Tom Kizzia (2013). Another book about a small place where larger forces meet. The piece is not in this register, but the discipline of staying with a single specific story while gesturing toward larger patterns is something I have learned from Kizzia.

## On craft

**The Journalist and the Murderer** by Janet Malcolm (1990). I re-read this every two years. The central claim — that the relationship between reporter and subject is, structurally, a relationship of betrayal — is uncomfortable and not entirely wrong. I think about Helen Fung when I think about Malcolm.

**The New New Journalism** edited by Robert Boynton (2005). A collection of interviews with longform reporters about their methods. Several of the interviews — particularly with Susan Orlean and with Adrian Nicole LeBlanc — have shaped how I approach my own source relationships.

**Tell It Slant** by Brenda Miller and Suzanne Paola (2003). Originally written as a textbook on creative nonfiction. The essay on structure in long pieces has helped me think about how to organize a 50,000-word investigation. The Mesa Verde piece does not have a single chronological spine. It has multiple spines that I am attempting to braid. *Tell It Slant* has clarified what I am doing without making me self-conscious about it.

## Pieces I have re-read for technique

- David Grann's *The Lost City of Z* (long magazine version, before the book)
- Ronan Farrow's reporting on the original Weinstein revelations
- Lauren Markham's reporting on the Twin Falls Idaho refugee case
- Patrick Radden Keefe's reporting on the Sackler family
- Several pieces by Suketu Mehta on Bombay, none of them about water but all of them models of how to render a place

## Pieces I have read and decided not to think too much about

Long features on western water that are, by my reading, structured as cinematic accounts of the West rather than as investigative work. There are many of them. They tend to be beautifully written. They tend to be, in some hard-to-pin-down way, content with the dramatic shape of the subject rather than with the specific facts of any given matter.

I do not want my piece to be a beautifully written account of the West. I want my piece to be an investigation that happens to take place in the West.

The distinction is, I think, the most important craft discipline I have imposed on myself. I have not always succeeded. The opening of chapter one is, in some lights, exactly the kind of writing I am trying to avoid. Reema noticed. She has not made me change it. She and I both know that the opening earns the right to be cinematic only if the rest of the piece, in its specificity, earns the trust of the reader.

If the rest of the piece does not earn it, the opening becomes a fault. I am writing toward earning it.
