# Verdant Corporate Structure Map

**Author:** Carlos Morales
**Status:** Working analysis; foundational to Chapter 4
**Date:** 2026-02-22 (significantly revised after Tucson visit with Helen Fung)

## Why this document

The corporate structure that Verdant Holdings built around the Mesa Verde acquisitions is, by design, opaque. It would be opaque to a casual researcher; it has been opaque, in part, to state regulators; it would be opaque to me without the careful work of mapping it across a dozen filings in three states.

This document is my map. It is not, and will not be, in the piece. The piece will describe the structure in narrative terms. The map exists here because, without it, the narrative would not be accurate.

## The structure at a high level

At the top of the structure is Verdant Holdings' parent entity, a Delaware-incorporated private equity firm. The firm is the ultimate owner. The firm's investors are a mix of pension funds, university endowments, and high-net-worth individuals. The firm's principals are Andrew Lacher and two other named partners.

Below the parent are several investment funds, each a Delaware limited partnership. The Mesa Verde acquisitions were made through one specific fund, which I will call here "the Fund." The Fund was the source of the capital. The Fund's investors are not publicly disclosed; they are the same general pool as the firm's overall investor base.

Below the Fund are sixteen wholly-owned subsidiary LLCs, each Delaware-registered. These LLCs were the entities that, on paper, purchased the ranching properties between 2014 and 2017. Each LLC was created for a specific cluster of acquisitions. The clustering does not, on its face, correspond to any meaningful organizational principle. The clustering is what one would expect if the purpose were to fragment the appearance of a single coordinated acquisition program.

Below the LLCs are the actual properties: 47 parcels totaling roughly 10,200 acres, across three New Mexico counties. The properties were purchased over a four-year period from 47 individual families.

Separately — and this is the structural innovation — the water-rights certificates that had been associated with the properties were not retained by the acquiring LLCs. They were assigned, by a series of separate documents executed at the time of each sale, to a different entity: a Delaware-registered holding company that did not own the underlying land. This holding company's stated purpose was the holding of water-rights assets.

The holding company was not, between 2014 and 2019, registered with the Mesa Verde Irrigation District. The district was not required to be notified of the assignments. The assignments were, in regulatory terms, invisible.

In 2019, the holding company applied to the district to consolidate its accumulated certificates into a single entity. The district approved. The state water board, after the 2021 memo and the modified vote in 2022, ratified.

By 2023, the consolidated entity began leasing portions of its water entitlement to agricultural operations in two adjacent states. The leases are the source of ongoing revenue. The valuations implied by those leases are the basis of the estimated $200 million to $400 million value of the Verdant Holdings water portfolio.

## What the structure accomplishes

Three things, in escalating order of significance:

**First**, it fragmented the public record. No single regulatory filing made the aggregation visible. No single county property record made the aggregation visible. No single state filing made the aggregation visible. To see the aggregation, a researcher had to cross-reference dozens of filings in three states.

**Second**, it separated the water rights from the land at the moment of acquisition. This separation made it possible to argue, later, that the water rights had been acquired in arms-length transactions that did not require the public comment and downstream effect analysis that would have applied to a single coordinated transfer of 47,400 acre-feet of water.

**Third**, it obscured the identity of the ultimate owner. The Mesa Verde Irrigation District, when it approved the consolidation in 2019, was not formally aware that the consolidating entity was a fund-controlled subsidiary of a Connecticut-based private equity firm. The district's bylaws did not require disclosure of ultimate beneficial ownership. The disclosure was not made.

## What I have done to verify the structure

- Pulled and reviewed every relevant filing with the Delaware Secretary of State, going back to 2014
- Pulled the property records for all 47 parcels from the three relevant county recorders' offices
- Obtained the Mesa Verde Irrigation District's records relating to the 2019 consolidation application, through Esther
- Cross-referenced the LLC officers and registered agents to identify shared corporate counsel
- Reviewed Helen Fung's account of the structure, which is consistent with my reconstruction in every detail
- Reviewed the structure with one of the three former Verdant employees, who confirmed it as accurate

## What the structure does not tell me

It does not tell me what Andrew Lacher believed he was doing. It does not tell me whether he understood the structure as a deliberate effort to evade public scrutiny, or whether he understood it as a defensible structuring of a complex transaction. I have my views about which is more likely. The piece will not assert which is true.

## How the piece will use this

Chapter 4 will describe the structure in approximately 1,800 words of narrative prose. The narrative will not, I hope, read like the schematic above. It will read like a story about decisions made by people in offices, decisions that compounded into a structure that produced the outcome the piece documents.

The map exists so that the narrative will be accurate. The map will not be on the page.
