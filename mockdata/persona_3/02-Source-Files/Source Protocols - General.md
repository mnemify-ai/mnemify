# Source Protocols — General

**Author:** Carlos Morales
**Status:** Living document, reviewed before every new source
**Date last updated:** 2026-05-01

## Why this document exists

I have been doing this work for twenty-one years. The rules I have developed for source protection are not the rules I was taught in journalism school. They are the rules I have arrived at after a series of mistakes, some small and some not small, and after watching colleagues make the larger versions of the same mistakes.

I write this document down because the rules will not stay in my head if I do not. I review it before I take on a new source. I have asked Reema to review it with me annually. The most recent review was in January 2026.

## The four levels of attribution, as I understand them

**On the record.** Everything is fair game. Name, title, exact quote. This is the default expectation when I introduce myself unless and until the source asks for a different arrangement.

**On background.** The information may be used. The source's name may not. A general descriptor (a former employee, a state official, an attorney with knowledge of the matter) is acceptable. The descriptor should not be specific enough to identify the source by process of elimination.

**Deep background.** The information may be used. No descriptor may be attached. The information must be confirmable through another source before it appears in print. This is the most fragile of the levels and the one I am most careful about.

**Off the record.** The information may not be used. It may inform my reporting and my questions. It does not appear in the piece, even in altered form, and it does not lead me to ask other sources questions that would only make sense if I knew it.

The four levels exist on a spectrum, and the spectrum is not always clear to sources. I am responsible for clarifying it at the start of every conversation.

## What I do at the start of a new source relationship

I introduce myself by full name and outlet. I explain what story I am working on, in language general enough not to compromise the story and specific enough that the source can give informed consent to participating.

I ask the source what level of attribution they want, before they have said anything substantive. If they have not thought about it, I walk them through the four levels. I tell them they can change their answer at any time before publication, and that I will honor any reasonable request for a stricter level at any point.

I take notes by hand. I do not record without explicit, repeated consent. When I do record, I tell the source they can ask me to stop at any time, and I will stop, and I will delete the recording if asked.

I tell the source I will, on request, read back any direct quote that I intend to use in the published piece before it appears. I do not offer prior review of the article as a whole. I have learned the hard way that prior review of the article as a whole creates problems for both reporter and source, and does not improve accuracy.

## What I do not do

I do not pay sources. I do not buy meals at venues I cannot justify on expense reports. I do not offer to keep information confidential that involves crimes against children, imminent threats of violence, or active medical emergencies. Sources are told this in advance.

I do not promise outcomes. I do not say "this will make a difference." I do not say "your story matters." Both of those are statements about the future that I cannot make. I say "thank you for talking to me" and I mean it.

I do not introduce sources to each other. I do not facilitate communication between sources. If two of my sources independently want to speak with each other, I will tell them so and let them find each other on their own.

I do not use information from one source to leverage another. I do not say to source B, "Source A told me X." I may say, "I have heard from another person familiar with the matter that X was the case." The difference is important.

## What I record about each source

A separate file per source, in encrypted storage. Each file contains: full name; preferred contact method; date of first contact; agreed level of attribution (with date and notes on any changes); summary of conversations; the source's own assessment of what they are risking by speaking with me; and any specific requests they have made about how their information may be used.

The file does not contain identifying information about other sources. I keep source relationships separated as a matter of operational hygiene. If my files are compromised — and they should be assumed to be, eventually, compromised — no single file should be sufficient to map out the full source network of any given story.

## What I keep at home and what I keep in the office

Nothing about active sources at home. My family lives in the house. Beatriz and Marina have not signed up to be participants in my work. The professional and the domestic stay separated by the door of my office in Mexico City, which has a lock.

Notes from active investigations travel with me in a single notebook that does not leave my person. I have been carrying Field Notes brand notebooks for fourteen years. The current notebook is the one in my jacket pocket as I write this.

## What I do if a source dies

This has happened to me three times. The first time, I did not know what to do, and I think I handled it badly. The second and third times I have followed the same protocol: I attend the funeral if I am welcome and able. I do not introduce myself to the family. I do not request access to the deceased's records. I assume that any commitments I made to the source remain in force after their death and that no agreement modifies them.

## What I review with Reema before publication

Every source above background level. The justification for naming each one. The risk assessment for each. The legal review notes for each. The source's most recent expressed preference for attribution. The reason any source has been moved to a more permissive level between first contact and publication.

I have, in my career, been wrong about a source's safety twice. Both times I learned about it after the piece had run. Neither situation resulted in harm to the source. I do not take this as evidence that my judgment is reliable. I take it as evidence that I have been lucky.
