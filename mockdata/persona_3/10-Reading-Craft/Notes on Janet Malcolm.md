# Notes on Janet Malcolm

**Author:** Carlos Morales
**Status:** Personal craft notes
**Date:** Various; this version consolidated 2026-05-01

## Why I keep returning to her

I have read *The Journalist and the Murderer* perhaps eight times. The first reading was in graduate school. The most recent was last month, in a single sitting, after I had finished the second draft of Chapter 5.

I do not read Malcolm because I agree with her. I read her because she makes me uncomfortable, and the discomfort is, I have come to believe, productive. She articulated, in 1990, a claim about the structure of journalism that I have been arguing with for thirty years and that I have not, in any final way, escaped.

The claim, in case I am ever in danger of forgetting it: *"Every journalist who is not too stupid or too full of himself to notice what is going on knows that what he does is morally indefensible."* The journalist, Malcolm wrote, betrays the subject by virtue of the structure of the relationship — by getting close enough to extract the material, by representing himself as something other than a betrayer, by writing what the subject did not consent to in the form in which it appears.

I have, in twenty-one years of work, found this claim partly true. I have also found it incomplete in ways that matter.

## What Malcolm gets right

She gets right that the relationship between a reporter and a source is structurally asymmetric. The reporter has the pen. The source has only the words they spoke. The reporter chooses which of the words appear, in what order, in what context, against what other material. The source has, in most cases, no equivalent power over the published piece.

She gets right that the seduction of the source is part of the work. The reporter who is good at the work is good at, in part, creating the conditions in which the source feels comfortable speaking. The conditions are not always conditions the source would have chosen if they had understood what was being made of them.

She gets right that the published piece will, in some cases, feel to the source like a betrayal. The feeling is not unreasonable. The source did not write the piece. The source contributed material that has been used in ways the source did not control.

## What I think Malcolm under-weights

She under-weights, I think, the legitimacy of the public interest that the work serves. Her argument is structured as a private moral matter — what one person does to another person — and is less attentive to what the published work does for the public that reads it.

In my reading of her, Malcolm treats the public interest as a kind of justification that journalists invoke to cover their structural complicity. She is, in some part of her argument, right. Journalists do invoke the public interest in self-serving ways. The invocation is not, however, always self-serving. Sometimes the public interest is real. Sometimes the public interest is the reason the source agreed to speak in the first place. Sometimes the public interest is the only thing that makes the work, in its full set of costs, defensible.

This is not, by my reading, a position Malcolm takes seriously. The book reads, in its central argument, as if all journalists are the same journalist, all interviews are the same interview, all betrayals are the same betrayal. The variations among us — variations of method, of motive, of relationship to the underlying material — are flattened.

I think the variations matter. I think the journalist who arrives at a source with a clear public interest in mind, and who explains the interest, and who keeps the explanation honest throughout the relationship, is doing different work from the journalist who arrives without an interest and constructs one later. The constructed interest is, I agree with Malcolm, often a justification. The pre-existing interest is, in my experience, something different.

## What I have learned from her anyway

I have learned that the awareness of the structural problem is, itself, a discipline. The journalist who is aware of the betrayal that the work risks is the journalist most likely to construct the relationship in a way that minimizes the betrayal. The journalist who refuses to recognize the problem is the journalist most likely to commit the betrayal in its fullest form.

I have learned that the relationship with each source is not the relationship with the previous source. The patterns that worked with one person may be the patterns that betray the next person. The discipline is in the specificity. Each source is encountered fresh.

I have learned that the published piece is not the relationship. The relationship ends when the piece appears. The piece, after that, has its own life. The source has to live with the piece, separately from having lived with me. I cannot, in writing the piece, mistake my own relationship with the source for the piece's relationship with the source. These are different things.

## What I am applying to the Mesa Verde piece

Helen Fung is the source for whom Malcolm's framework is most acute. I have, with Helen, the relationship that Malcolm describes most pointedly: a relationship of unusual intimacy, in which the source has told me things she has not told other people, in which the conditions of the telling have included the warmth of my presence and her own willingness to be vulnerable, in which the piece will use what she has given me in ways she did not author.

I am, with Helen, doing what I can to minimize the betrayal. I am letting her see her quoted material in advance. I am letting her negotiate language. I am writing the chapter in a way that, I hope, allows her to be a full person rather than a useful character.

I cannot, however, escape the structural fact. The chapter is mine. The piece is mine. Helen has consented to her participation in it, but she has not consented to my judgments about what her participation means. The judgments will be the piece's, not hers. The reader will read the piece, not the conversation we had in Tucson.

This is the cost. I have decided, after many readings of Malcolm, that the cost is paid in service of something I am willing to defend. The piece's value to the public, on my best reading, exceeds the cost to Helen. The cost to Helen is, however, real. I will not pretend it is not.

## What Malcolm would say about my piece

I do not know. She is dead. She wrote *The Journalist and the Murderer* in 1990 and continued to write, on adjacent matters, until her death in 2021. Her later writing, I think, was less categorical than the early formulation. She allowed, in the later work, for variations.

If she could read my piece, I imagine she would find things to admire and things to criticize. She would, I suspect, criticize my treatment of Helen Fung in particular. She would say that the warmth of the rendering is itself a kind of seduction — that the reader's sympathy for Helen, which the piece earns through the care of the prose, is a sympathy the source did not author and that the source may not have intended.

I would, I think, accept the criticism partially. The sympathy is a result of the prose. I am responsible for the prose. Helen did not write it. The sympathy belongs to me and to the reader.

Whether this means I have betrayed Helen, in Malcolm's sense, is a question I do not have a settled answer to. I have read the question. I have considered it. I have made the choice I have made. I am, in writing this craft note, recording that I made the choice with awareness of what the choice cost.

This is, I think, the most a working journalist can do with Malcolm's framework. Not refuse the work. Not pretend the cost is absent. Do the work, knowing the cost, and write the cost down, somewhere, in case anyone ever asks.
