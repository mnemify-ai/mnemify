# Notes on *Killers of the Flower Moon*

**Author:** Carlos Morales
**Status:** Personal craft notes
**Date:** 2026-03-14

## Why I have re-read it three times this year

I have been working on the Mesa Verde piece for eighteen months. In the past year I have re-read David Grann's *Killers of the Flower Moon* three times. The first re-read was in November 2024, when the piece was first taking shape and I needed to remember what investigative writing at the highest level looks like. The second was in May 2025, after I had finished a difficult interview with Hortensia Sandoval and needed to think about how Grann had handled comparably weighted material. The third was in February 2026, on the plane back from Tucson, when I was carrying Helen Fung's voice in my head and needed to think about how Grann allowed his sources to be themselves on the page.

I do not re-read books often. I have re-read *Killers of the Flower Moon* three times in a year because the book is, for the work I am doing, the closest thing I have to a model.

## What the book is

Grann's investigation, in book form, of the Osage murders of the 1920s. The murders, by the standard accounts I had read before opening the book, were a Northwest Oklahoma horror story: members of the Osage Nation, made wealthy by oil discoveries on tribal land, killed in series by white people who married into Osage families or otherwise positioned themselves to inherit the headrights that flowed from oil revenues. The murders are a historical fact. The federal investigation that eventually addressed them, conducted by what became the FBI, is also a historical fact. The book recounts both.

What the book does that previous accounts had not done is shift the moral center of the narrative away from the federal investigation and toward the Osage themselves. The first two parts of the book are written from the Osage perspective, with Mollie Burkhart at the moral center. The third part of the book is the part I have been re-reading most carefully — Grann's own present-day investigation into Osage deaths that the original federal investigation did not address.

The third part is, in some way, what made the book a model for my own work. It is the part in which the contemporary reporter enters the story. It is the part in which Grann acknowledges the limits of his own access, the partiality of his own findings, the existence of additional injustices that the book is not in a position to fully document.

## What I take from his structure

Three things.

**First**, the discipline of telling the story from the perspective of the people the story was done to, not the perspective of the people who eventually addressed it. The federal investigation is, in conventional terms, the dramatic spine of the Osage story. Grann does not let the investigation be the spine. The Osage are the spine. The investigation is what happens in the gap that the Osage forced open by their grief and their pressure.

The Mesa Verde piece, I have been trying to write, with the families at the center rather than with the state water board's eventual ratification at the center. The Sandoval chapter, the Esther chapter — these are the chapters that carry the moral weight. The chapters on Lacher and on the corporate structure are the chapters about the mechanism. The mechanism is necessary. The mechanism is not the story.

**Second**, the willingness to render the perpetrators carefully, without flattening them into villainy. Grann's portrait of William Hale — the most directly culpable figure in the Osage murders — is not a portrait of pure evil. Hale is rendered as a specific person, with specific calculations, in a specific historical and economic context. The reader understands what he did. The reader also understands, in some smaller way, the world in which what he did was possible.

I have been trying, with Andrew Lacher, to do the equivalent restraint. Lacher is not Hale. The cases are not the same. But the principle of careful rendering, of refusing the easy demonization that would make the reader feel virtuous, is the principle I am trying to honor. The reader of the Mesa Verde piece should finish Chapter 4 understanding what Lacher did and also understanding the world in which what he did was possible. The second understanding is the more difficult writing problem.

**Third**, the third part. The reporter who enters the story late, who acknowledges the partiality of the inherited account, who continues the investigation past the formal closure. This is the move I have been thinking about for the Mesa Verde piece's closing.

The piece will not, like Grann's book, run hundreds of pages on a contemporary investigation. The piece is a magazine investigation, not a book. But the closing chapter, in some way, can do what Grann's third part does in its smaller register. The closing can acknowledge the partiality. The closing can name what the piece has not addressed. The closing can locate the published material as one moment in a longer accounting.

I have been writing the closing in this register. I am not yet sure I have succeeded. The closing of Chapter 8 is the closing I have written. It may not be the closing the piece needs.

## What I learn from the prose

The restraint. The willingness to let a scene be a scene. The trust that the reader will understand what is at stake without being told.

Grann's prose is, for an investigative reporter, deceptively plain. The sentences are simple. The paragraphs are short. The complexity of the underlying material is allowed to be the complexity. The prose does not try to add complexity that the material does not contain. The prose does not try to add drama that the events do not produce on their own.

I have been trying to write Mesa Verde in this register. I have, in places, succeeded. The opening of Chapter 1 — the description of Lake Talavera in the 1960s photograph, then the description of what is there now — is the kind of writing I learned from re-reading Grann. The scene with the rose at the depression where the lake was, which is the closing of Chapter 8, is the kind of writing I am trying for. I do not yet know whether the scene works. Reema has not yet seen the final version.

## What Grann's book does that mine cannot

His book has scale. The murders he documents took place over years and involved many people. The institutional response was federal in scope. The historical context is the full history of Osage oil wealth and the broader pattern of land theft from Indigenous peoples in Oklahoma and beyond.

My piece is, by comparison, a small story. One irrigation district. Forty-seven families. One private equity firm. One particular legal mechanism. The piece is, in some way, the inverse of Grann's: I am writing about a specific transactional structure that operated quietly over five years and that did not produce a single dramatic moment.

This is the writing challenge I have been working on. The piece is, on its face, lower-stakes than Grann's. The piece is, in its underlying argument, about a pattern that recurs in the contemporary West and that, in its cumulative effect across many similar matters, may be more consequential than any single dramatic crime. The Mesa Verde matter is one instance of a general pattern. The piece has to make the general pattern visible through the specific instance.

This is, by my reading, harder than what Grann did. Grann had a clear crime. I have a clear practice. The practice, I think, is in the long run more important than the crime. The practice is also harder to write about.

I am, in some way, trying to extend Grann's method into a different kind of material. Whether the extension works is what the piece will, when it is published, reveal.

## The line I keep coming back to

In *Killers of the Flower Moon*, Grann describes, in the closing pages, his encounter with an elderly Osage woman who has lived with the consequences of the murders her entire life. He records what she said to him. He does not editorialize on what she said. He lets the line stand.

I will not, in these notes, transcribe the line. The line belongs to Grann's book. What I will say is that the line, in its simplicity, is the line that the entire book has been earning the right to print. The reader, when they reach the line, knows what it means. They know what the woman has lost. They know what the institutional response has not restored. They know that the writer has come to her, late, and asked her to speak, and that she has spoken because someone needed to.

This is, I think, what I am trying to do with Hortensia's line in Chapter 8 — the line about her grandfather, about the lake. The line is simple. The line, if the piece works, will be the line the piece has been earning the right to print.

If the line works, the piece will work. If the line does not work, I will not have done the work I have been trying for.

I will know, when the piece is in the reader's hands, whether the line works. I do not yet know. I will not know until I cannot change anything.

This is, in the end, what writing is. The not-knowing, until you cannot change anything. The submission of the work, finally, to the reader. The willingness to be wrong about what you have made.

I have, after twenty-one years, accepted this. I have not, in twenty-one years, gotten used to it.
