# Trip Notes — The Drive to Lake Talavera

**Date:** April 19, 2026
**Logged from notebook same day**

---

I picked up Hortensia and Yolanda at 7:30 in the morning. Hortensia was already on the porch when I arrived. She was wearing a dark blue dress and had pulled her white hair back. Yolanda was at the door, calling something back into the house, probably to her own children.

The cooler was already packed. Hortensia had made tamales the night before — *para el camino*, she said — and Yolanda had made the horchata. There was a thermos of coffee that Yolanda had filled at four in the morning, before Hortensia woke up, because Yolanda knew her mother would want coffee on the drive and would refuse to make it.

Yolanda drove. Hortensia sat in the passenger seat. I sat in the back, where I could see them both in the rearview mirror.

We did not talk much for the first hour. The road was empty. The light was the early light, blue and silver, that comes just before the sun rises high enough to bake the asphalt. Hortensia looked out the window. Yolanda glanced at her, every so often, in a way that I noticed and that Hortensia did not.

At 8:14 Yolanda turned on the radio. KRZE, out of Albuquerque. Ranchera music from the 1970s. The first song was José Alfredo Jiménez, *El Rey*. I do not know if she chose the station deliberately, but if she did, the choice was perfect. Hortensia closed her eyes for a moment and her mouth moved a little, mouthing along with the lyrics. She did not sing. She mouthed.

I did not write this down at the time. I am writing it now, that evening, in the hotel room in Belen.

---

We stopped for gasoline outside of Socorro. Yolanda paid. Hortensia stayed in the truck. I went into the convenience store to buy bottled water. The clerk was a man in his sixties who looked at me with the kind of professional patience that long-haul gas stations require. I asked him for directions to the access road to the old lake bed. He said: "There's nothing out there." I said: "I know." He gave me the directions.

---

We arrived at the depression at 11:42. The drive from Belen to the coordinates was about three and a half hours, with the stop. The temperature was already 88 degrees. The wind was from the southwest, light.

Yolanda parked the truck on the dirt road that had once led to the dock. The dock is gone. The road is now a track that ends about three hundred yards from where the dock was. We walked the last three hundred yards. Hortensia walked slowly. Yolanda offered her arm. Hortensia accepted.

The depression is about three hundred yards across at its widest point. The clay floor is cracked in irregular polygons, the way old clay cracks. There are skeletal cottonwoods around the edge. Some of them are still upright. Most have fallen. The fallen ones lie in directions that suggest the prevailing wind has not been consistent over the years.

There is no marker. There is no sign indicating what the place once was. There is no fencing. There is nothing.

Hortensia walked to the edge of the depression. She paused. Then she walked down. The descent is not steep. Maybe ten feet down from the rim to the floor. She walked down carefully and Yolanda walked behind her and I walked behind them both.

When we reached the floor Hortensia walked to what I would estimate was the rough center of the depression. She had been carrying something I had not noticed. A single white rose. She had had it in her hand, perhaps in her coat pocket, the whole drive.

She set the rose on the cracked clay floor.

She stood for what I noted at the time as one minute and forty-two seconds. I counted, in my head, because counting was a way to refuse the temptation to do anything else.

Then she said, quietly, more to herself than to us: "Mi abuelo trajo a mi abuela aquí. Antes de casarse. Le dijo que quería mostrarle un lago en el desierto. Ella no le creyó."

[My grandfather brought my grandmother here. Before they were married. He told her he wanted to show her a lake in the desert. She did not believe him.]

She turned and walked back up to the rim. Yolanda took her arm again. I followed.

---

We drove back to Belen mostly in silence. Yolanda did not turn the radio back on. Hortensia looked out the window. The light by now was the high desert light, white and unsparing.

Once, somewhere between San Antonio and Bernardo, Hortensia said: "He brought my grandmother there in nineteen-twenty. There was a lake then. He had not been lying."

She said it in English, possibly for my benefit. I think now she said it because she wanted me to write it down. I did. It is the second-to-last line of Chapter 8.

---

Hortensia thanked me when we got back to her house. She had me come inside for water before I went on to the hotel. We sat at her kitchen table. The water had been from the refrigerator and was very cold. She drank a glass and then said: "I do not know if I will go back. I think I will not. The visit was for me to go once. I have gone once. That is enough."

I said I understood.

She said: "When you write about the trip, please do not make me sound like I was crying. I was not crying."

I said I would not.

She said: "I am old enough to know that the lake is gone. The rose was not for the lake. The rose was for what I had been told the lake was, by my grandfather, who is also gone. The losses do not stack on top of each other. They are not the same loss. I want the writing to know the difference."

I said I would try to make the writing know the difference.

She said: "I will read it before it is published. You promised me."

I said yes, I had promised her, and yes I would.

We sat for another few minutes. Yolanda came in. Hortensia stood, slowly, and walked me to the door. I drove to the hotel. I have been here, writing this, for the last four hours.

---

**Note added later that night:**

I do not always write field notes this long after a trip. Tonight I am writing them long because I do not know what the right length is, and because I am trying to keep the day on the page before it leaves me.

I have, in twenty-one years, made perhaps eight or nine drives like this with sources. They are the days I remember when I am back in my office in Mexico City and the work becomes abstract. The days like this one are the days when the work is not abstract.

The rose. The mouthing along to the radio. *El Rey*. The light by the gas station outside Socorro. The clerk who knew there was nothing out there. The 88 degrees. The 102 minutes between when we arrived at the depression and when we drove away. The difference, as Hortensia named it, between the loss of the lake and the loss of what she had been told the lake was.

The piece will not be able to carry all of these. I will keep the rose. I will keep what Hortensia said about her grandfather. I will keep, I think, the radio. I will not keep the clerk at the gas station. I will not keep the 102 minutes.

The day will, in the end, become approximately 700 words on the page. The 700 words will be the final scene of the piece. The 700 words will not be the day. The day was what it was. The 700 words are what I have made of the day.

This is, I think, the limit of what writing does. The day was the day. The writing is what I can do with the day.

I do not yet know whether I can do enough.
