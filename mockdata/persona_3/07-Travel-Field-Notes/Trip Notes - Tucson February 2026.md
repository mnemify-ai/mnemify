# Trip Notes — Tucson, Arizona

**Dates:** February 2-4, 2026
**Purpose:** Two-day in-person session with Helen Fung
**Logged from notebook 2026-02-02 through 2026-02-04**

---

**February 2 (Monday).**

Drove from Mexico City. Three days. Stopped in Hermosillo the first night, in Tucson via a motel outside Nogales the second. Arrived at Helen's house at 9:42 in the morning. Knocked. Heard a cat on the other side of the door. She opened the door and looked at me for a long moment before she said: "Come in. The coffee is bad. I made it anyway."

I have written about that sentence elsewhere. It is the sentence women of a particular generation in this part of the country use to receive strangers. I do not believe Helen and Esther have ever met or spoken with each other. The sentence is older than either of them.

The house is small. Adobe-style, on a quiet street near the university. Two cats, both gray, both old. Native plants in the front yard. A garden in the back. The interior is austere and well-kept. Books, but not many. A single black-and-white photograph on the wall above the dining table, of a man in a draftsman's coat — her father, I would learn later in the day.

I had brought, on Reema's suggestion, a small gift: a book on architecture in Phoenix that I had picked up at a used bookstore in El Paso. Helen accepted the book. She set it on a side table. She did not look at it during the day.

The first session lasted approximately three hours. We worked at the dining table. She made coffee twice. She offered lunch; I accepted; we ate sandwiches she had made the night before. The conversation moved in a particular pattern that I have, since, recognized as the pattern of an attorney who has been preparing for a hostile deposition for three years. She would not let me direct. She had decided, before I arrived, what she would tell me on the first day. She told me, in roughly the order she had decided.

At one point in the afternoon I asked her a question that, judging by her brief silence, she had not anticipated. The question was: "How long have you been planning what you would say to me?"

She paused for what I noted at the time as twelve seconds. Then she said: "Since I retired. Three years."

She did not elaborate. I did not press. I have learned, with sources of her caliber, that the most consequential things will be said when they decide. The pressing produces ordinary things. The waiting produces the extraordinary.

We finished at 5:30. I drove to the motel. I did not eat dinner. I went over my notes for two hours and then slept badly.

**February 3 (Tuesday).**

Returned at 9:00. The second day was different. The decided narrative had been delivered the first day. The second day was the day she had not, before I arrived, planned in detail.

She made coffee. We sat. She did not begin. I did not press. For perhaps four minutes we drank coffee in silence.

Then she said: "I have been thinking, since you left yesterday, about whether I have been honest with you."

I waited.

"I have been honest about the things I have told you," she said. "I have not been honest about the things I have not told you."

That was the beginning of the second day. She spoke for the rest of the morning about her father. I have notes on the conversation in a separate file. The notes are extensive. The conversation was, on its face, about Helen's father, who had come to the United States from Hong Kong in 1957 and had worked as a draftsman for an architectural firm in Phoenix until his death in 1988.

The conversation was, in its substance, about Helen's reasons for choosing the law as a profession and for choosing the specialty she eventually arrived at. The conversation was, in its deepest layer, an extended meditation on the question of whether her father would, if he had lived to see what she did with her career, have been proud of her.

She did not, in the entire conversation, give me an answer to that question. She did not, I think, have an answer.

In the afternoon, we returned to the Mesa Verde matter, but the conversation was different. Helen was, by that point, no longer simply explaining what she had done. She was, by my reading, asking me — and asking herself — whether what she had done was defensible. The question of legality was settled. The question of rightness was what she was, in the afternoon, willing to entertain.

I have transcribed selections from the afternoon in a separate file. They include the line: "I have spent the last three years asking myself whether 'legal' was the question that mattered."

This line is the line the piece will, I think, rest on.

We finished at 6:30. She offered me dinner. I accepted. We ate at a small Mexican restaurant near her house. The food was good. We talked about her cats and about Marina and about the way the light falls on the mountains in February. We did not, during dinner, discuss the matter.

**February 4 (Wednesday).**

Brief morning visit. Approximately ninety minutes. We confirmed what we had agreed to during the previous two days. She asked me to read back specific passages I might quote. I read three. She approved two and asked me to alter the wording on the third. I have altered it.

We discussed the practical matter of fact-checking. She has agreed that Kayla may call her and that she will confirm anything Kayla asks her to confirm. She has asked, separately, that any conversations between her and Kayla be recorded by Kayla and made available to her if she ever needs them. I have communicated this to Kayla.

We discussed, briefly, the possibility of her speaking with another journalist after publication. I told her that her decisions in this regard are entirely her own and that I have no claim on her further public engagement. She thanked me.

I left at 11:00. The drive back to Mexico City took four days because I stopped to make additional reporting calls along the way.

---

**CM note added several months later:**

I have, in two decades of investigative reporting, perhaps three days of conversation that have changed me. The two days with Helen Fung in February 2026 are two of those days.

She is a source. She is also, by some other measure I do not know how to articulate, a person who has thought more carefully about the moral content of professional life than almost anyone I have ever interviewed. She is sixty-six years old and is, by her own description, retired. She is also, in my reading, working harder than most people I know. She is working on the question of what her work amounted to.

I do not know what she will conclude. I do not know whether the conclusion will be public. I have promised her that if she ever wishes to speak with me again, my notebook will be available. She has not said whether she will.

The piece, when it appears, will treat her with the respect she has earned. It will not flatter her. It will not condemn her. It will let her speak for herself, in language I have read back to her, with characterizations she has approved.

This is, I think, the most I can offer a source like Helen. I have done what I can do. The rest is hers.
