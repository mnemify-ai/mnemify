# Trip Notes — Mesilla, New Mexico

**Dates:** November 11-19, 2024
**Purpose:** Originally a follow-on to the labor piece; became, by the end of the trip, the beginning of this investigation
**Logged from notebooks 2024-11-12 through 2024-11-19**

---

**November 11 (Monday).**

Flew from Mexico City to El Paso. The plane was late by an hour because of weather at El Paso that I never saw evidence of. Rented a car. Drove north through Las Cruces and into Mesilla. The road from Las Cruces to Mesilla is six miles and is one of the most-traveled six miles in this part of the state, and is, in November, the color of a brown paper bag that has been left in the sun for a season.

The light here is different from the light in Mexico City. In Mexico City the light is always partly thrown back at you from concrete. Here it falls on dirt and is absorbed. I had forgotten this. I had not been in this part of New Mexico in eight years.

Checked into a small hotel in Mesilla that I had stayed at before in 2016. The same owners. They did not remember me. I did not remind them.

**November 12 (Tuesday).**

Met with Tomás Aragón at 10:00. The original purpose of the trip. Tomás runs a small workers' rights organization in the area and has been organizing among workers at the fruit-packing facility that was my reason for the trip. We talked for three hours. The labor piece is real. The conditions are bad. I will write it. I told Tomás I will write it.

Tomás introduced me, over lunch, to a friend of his named Adela Reyes. Adela is sixty-six and is, I gathered over the lunch, a retired schoolteacher whose husband had once worked as a surveyor and who now spends her days reading and gardening and writing letters to her grandchildren in Albuquerque. We talked about books. She is reading, currently, a Joan Didion biography in Spanish translation. We talked about Didion in two languages without really agreeing on whether she was a great writer or merely an interesting one.

Adela mentioned, in passing, that her neighbor down the street had been on the state water board and had resigned in 2022. I asked, also in passing, what the neighbor had thought of the work. Adela said: "She did not say much. She has been quiet since then."

I made a note of the neighbor's name. I did not mention it to Tomás. I did not, that day, do anything with the note.

**November 13 (Wednesday).**

At the fruit-packing facility, interviewing three workers Tomás had introduced me to. The interviews were difficult. The workers were nervous. We spoke in Spanish. The conversations took longer than they should have because they kept testing whether I was who I said I was and whether the magazine was what I said it was. I do not blame them. I would have done the same.

That evening I walked the streets of Mesilla after dinner. I passed the house of Adela's neighbor — the former state water board member — twice. The lights were on. I did not knock.

The right time to make first contact with a source is the time when you have done enough reading to know what questions to ask. I had not done enough reading yet. I had read newspaper coverage of her resignation. I had read the public statements from the water board around that time. I had not read the underlying memos. I did not know enough to walk to her door.

**November 14 (Thursday).**

In the morning, drove to Las Cruces and spent four hours at the NMSU library, reading the public record on the State Water Resources Board between 2019 and 2022. There was less of a public record than I had expected. The board had, during this period, taken few formal actions. The actions it had taken were described in the local press in technical language that, on first reading, did not yield any obvious story.

I read the press coverage of the June 2022 vote ratifying the Mesa Verde consolidation. The coverage was four paragraphs in the *Las Cruces Sun-News*. The coverage did not mention the resignation of the board member three months earlier. The coverage did not mention the prior representation by the general counsel. The coverage was, on its face, a routine government story.

I read it three times. Each reading made me more suspicious. A piece of routine government coverage that does not mention obvious context is, in my experience, the most reliable signal that there is context the coverage decided not to mention.

In the afternoon, I drove back to Mesilla and walked past the house again. Knocked. She answered the door. I introduced myself by full name and outlet. I said I was working on a piece about water in the region and that I had read the press coverage of her resignation and wanted to know if she would be willing to speak with me. She looked at me for a long moment. She said: "Come in. The coffee is bad. I made it anyway."

That sentence, almost word for word, would later appear in a different setting, from a different source — Helen Fung — and I would be reminded that there are sentences that are not original to any one person. They are the sentences of a region. Helen Fung said it. Esther had said it first. I think now, looking back at my notes, that the sentence is the sentence of women of a certain generation in this part of the country, who have made coffee for people they were not sure they wanted in their kitchens, for many decades.

**November 15 (Friday).**

Second conversation with Esther. This time longer. Three hours. I made notes by hand. I did not ask to record. I would ask, in our third meeting, and she would agree.

By the end of the meeting I understood that I was no longer in New Mexico for the labor piece. The labor piece would continue. I would write it. But it would not be the major thing this trip had produced.

I called Reema that night from the hotel. I did not pitch the new piece. I said only: "I have found something. I will tell you when I get back."

She said: "Take notes."

**November 16-19 (Saturday-Tuesday).**

Field reporting on the labor piece, as planned. Three additional worker interviews. A visit to the corporate office of the fruit-packing operation in El Paso. A site visit to the loading area at the packing facility. The labor piece will be solid; the reporting was productive.

I made, in my off-hours, several phone calls about the new piece. I read NMSU's library records on water-rights certificates. I began to understand the doctrine of prior appropriation in a way I had not previously.

By Tuesday night I had a working hypothesis about what had happened in the Mesa Verde district. I did not yet have evidence for the hypothesis. I had Esther's word for what she had seen.

**Flying back, November 20.**

I sat in the El Paso airport waiting for my plane and I wrote, in my notebook, a single sentence that I have come back to many times since:

*The hardest stories to write are the stories where no one broke a rule.*

I did not, then, know what the sentence meant. I think now that the entire piece is, in some way, an attempt to figure out what the sentence means.

---

**CM note added in 2026:**

I am re-reading these notes eighteen months after I wrote them. The sentence about the coffee. The sentence about no one breaking a rule. The slow recognition that the piece I had come to write was not the piece I was going to write.

This is the texture of the work. It does not announce itself. It accumulates in conversations that begin as something else.

Adela's casual mention of her neighbor was the beginning of the entire investigation. Adela does not know this. I have, in the eighteen months since, not been back to Mesilla, in part because I do not want to draw attention to the connection between Adela and Esther. They live on the same street.

I will, when the piece publishes, send Adela a copy. I will not tell her she was the start of it.
