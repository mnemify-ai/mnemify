# Interview Excerpt — Helen Fung, Day 1

**Date:** 2026-02-03
**Location:** Helen Fung's home, Tucson, Arizona
**Duration:** Approximately 5 hours across morning and afternoon sessions
**Recording:** Yes (with consent)
**Transcribed by:** CM
**This excerpt:** Approximately hour 3 of the day, after lunch

---

CM: You said this morning that everything was legal. I want to ask you the next question, which is: was anything wrong?

HELEN: That is the question. That is exactly the question, Carlos. And I have to tell you that I have not arrived at an answer that I am willing to defend in public.

CM: Are you willing to tell me what you have arrived at?

HELEN: I am willing to tell you what I have arrived at, with the understanding that if I see my own arrival at it written in your magazine in language that does not preserve the distinctions I am about to draw, I will be unhappy with you.

CM: I understand.

HELEN: Good. Here is what I think. The transactions I structured for Mr. Lacher's firm were, in the narrow sense, lawful. Every step was within the rules. No rule was broken.

But there was a question, in each transaction, that I did not ask. The question was: should this be lawful? Not whether it was lawful, which I confirmed, but whether the framework that made it lawful was a framework I was willing to participate in.

That question is, I now think, the question an attorney has to ask. It is not part of the formal training. It is not part of the ethical rules. The ethical rules are about your conduct within the framework. They are not about your participation in the framework itself.

I did not ask the question. I should have asked the question. I do not know what answer I would have arrived at if I had asked it. I suspect that I would have arrived at the same conclusion — that the framework was the framework, that my refusal to participate in it would have been replaced by someone else's willingness to participate in it, that the outcome would have been the same and only the attorney would have been different. That is a defense. I think it is also a lie I tell myself.

CM: Why do you think it's a lie?

HELEN: Because the framework only exists if attorneys participate in it. Each individual attorney's refusal would not change anything. The collective refusal of every attorney who was asked to participate would change everything. I know this. I have always known this. The argument that "someone else would do it" is the argument every individual person in every collective failure has made, since the beginning of professional practice. It is what doctors said about helping with executions. It is what accountants said about books that did not add up. It is what attorneys say about every framework that requires their cooperation to produce its outcomes.

I told myself the argument. The argument was true at the level of the individual transaction. It was a lie at the level of the system.

CM: When did you start telling yourself it was a lie?

HELEN: I will tell you something, Carlos. I started telling myself it was a lie at the end. After I retired. Before I retired, I did not think about it. I worked. I billed my hours. I went home to my cats. I told myself the work was complicated and that I was doing it well, and the complication itself was a kind of moral cover.

When I retired, I had time. And in the time, I began to think about my father, who chose his profession because of its protections. And I began to ask myself what I had been protecting, and from whom.

I did not arrive at an answer that I am at peace with. I think I will not arrive at one. I will continue to ask the question, and the question will continue to be the answer.

CM: That sounds like something you have rehearsed.

HELEN: I have rehearsed it because I knew you were going to ask me the question. I am not telling you something I have not thought about. I am telling you something I have thought about every day for three years.

CM: Is that the same thing as honest?

HELEN: I have not decided.

[She paused for some time. I did not fill the silence. We then went on to discuss her father, which is in a separate excerpt file.]

**CM notes after this session:**

Helen Fung is the source of a lifetime. Twenty-one years of reporting and I have not had a conversation like this.

The danger is the danger I have written about elsewhere in these files: that I will let her framing become the article's framing. She is sufficiently articulate, sufficiently self-aware, sufficiently morally serious, that the reader will be inclined to accept her self-assessment. I have to bring my own judgment to bear.

My judgment, having now spent two days with her, is that her self-assessment is mostly accurate, but it is mostly accurate in a way that is itself a function of her professional skill. She has constructed a careful, defensible, partial confession. It is the confession she has decided to make. There are aspects of what she did that she has not addressed even with herself, and that I am not, in two days of conversation, going to surface.

The piece will have to acknowledge this. The reader has to understand that what Helen has given me is what Helen has decided to give. The fact that what she has given is unusually candid does not mean it is exhaustive.

This is the most subtle problem in the entire investigation. I do not yet know how I will write around it.
