# Interview Excerpt — Verdant Former Employee 2

**Date:** 2025-10-19
**Location:** A coffee shop in [redacted city], not in any state where the source has lived or worked
**Duration:** 3 hours 12 minutes

[Excerpt begins after we had established the basic framework of the source's tenure at Verdant Holdings and the source had agreed that I could take notes.]

EMPLOYEE 2: You want to know what kind of place it was. I will tell you.

It was the kind of place where the smartest person in every room was Lacher, and everybody knew it, and everybody acted accordingly. He did not need to assert it. He carried it.

You would meet with him about a transaction. He would have read the documents. He would have memorized the documents. He would ask you a question that started in the middle of paragraph seventeen of a forty-page memo. You would either be ready for the question or you would not. If you were not, you did not get a second chance.

CM: What kind of person was he in those meetings?

EMPLOYEE 2: Polite. Quiet. Precise. He never raised his voice. He did not make jokes. He did not, that I ever saw, lose his composure. If a junior person made an error, he would say, "Help me understand how you arrived at that," and he would wait. The waiting was worse than yelling. The waiting made you understand that you had failed to think.

I admired him at the time. I still admire some things about him. I would not work for him again.

CM: Why not?

EMPLOYEE 2: Because what I was doing while I admired him was helping him acquire a thing that did not belong to him for less than it was worth. I did not know, at the time, that this is what I was doing. I knew that we were buying ranchland and we were structuring the water rights into separate entities. I had been told that this was a sophisticated approach to a complex regulatory environment. I had not been told that the sellers did not understand what they were selling.

CM: Did Lacher know?

EMPLOYEE 2: Yes.

CM: How do you know he knew?

EMPLOYEE 2: I was in a meeting, in the spring of 2017, in which one of the firm's principals — not Lacher — asked Lacher whether the sellers understood the value of what they were transferring. Lacher said, in language I have not forgotten: "They understand the value of the price. They have not been asked to understand the value of the asset. That is a different question. The asset is structured in such a way that they will not be asked."

The principal nodded. The meeting continued. I made a note of the language in my own files. I do not have the notes anymore. I am giving you the language as I remember it.

CM: Do you remember it accurately?

EMPLOYEE 2: I remember it accurately. I have thought about that sentence every week since 2017. The cadence of it. The construction. "They have not been asked to understand the value of the asset. That is a different question." I have asked myself, since then, whether the difference between asking and not asking is the entire difference between a fair transaction and an unfair one. I have concluded that it is.

You will use that quote, Carlos. I am giving it to you because it is the cleanest description of what Lacher built. I am asking you to use it without attribution to me. There were a small number of people in that meeting. I cannot be identifiable.

CM: I will use language consistent with our agreement. I will describe the source as someone present at the firm during the relevant period.

EMPLOYEE 2: That is fine. There will be at least eight people who could be the source by that description. I am one of them. The other seven will not say anything to anyone. The protection is sufficient.

[End of selected excerpt. Subsequent discussion concerned the firm's internal communications discipline, which I have notes on separately.]

**CM notes after this session:**

The Lacher quote is, if it can be verified or at least corroborated, the central piece of attribution in the entire piece. It establishes — in his own voice, even if reported — that he was aware of the asymmetry of information between buyer and seller.

I have, since this conversation, taken three steps:

1. I have asked Employee 2 to walk me through the meeting in greater detail in subsequent conversations. They have. The details are consistent.
2. I have asked Employee 1 and Employee 3 whether they ever heard Lacher use similar language. Employee 1 says yes, in different words, at a different time. Employee 3 says no but believes the characterization is accurate.
3. I have asked Helen Fung whether she ever heard Lacher discuss the question of seller understanding. She has declined to answer this question, on grounds of client confidentiality. Her decline is itself, in its own careful way, a piece of information.

I believe the quote is accurate. I will use it. I will use it with the attribution we have agreed: "an individual who was present at Verdant Holdings during the relevant period." Lacher will, almost certainly, be asked to comment on the quote before publication. I do not expect him to comment. If he does, his comment will be reported.

This is the kind of moment in an investigation where the reporter's judgment is what stands between a defensible piece and an undefended assertion. I have to be willing, in a deposition, to defend my reliance on this source. I am. I have spent thirteen hours with this person across three meetings. Their account is consistent, specific, and corroborated. The piece will use the quote.
