# Interview Excerpt — Esther, Session 7

**Date:** 2026-01-22
**Location:** Esther's kitchen, Mesilla, New Mexico
**Duration:** 2 hours 14 minutes
**Recording:** Yes (with consent, both at start and reconfirmed at minute 47)
**Transcribed by:** CM
**This excerpt:** Approximately minutes 31 through 58 of the session

---

CM: I want to come back to something you said in our last meeting. You said the memo, when you read it the third time, was when you understood. What did you understand?

ESTHER: I understood that someone had decided we were going to vote a particular way before the memo was written. The memo was constructed in such a way that voting any other way would require a board member to refuse to accept the staff's analysis. And the staff's analysis was, on its face, professional. It had citations. It had the right legal language. To vote against it, you had to be willing to say, in public, that you thought the staff had been corrupted. That's a different kind of vote.

CM: Did you believe the staff had been corrupted?

ESTHER: I believed the staff had been... I think the word I would use is "instrumentalized." That's not the same as corrupted. The staff wasn't lying. The staff was doing what staff does when staff has been given a question with a desired answer. They had assembled the materials that supported the desired answer. They had not assembled the materials that would have complicated it. That's a kind of corruption, but it's a polite kind.

CM: Who had given the staff the desired answer?

ESTHER: I have an opinion about that.

CM: Will you tell me?

ESTHER: Not for the piece. Not yet. Maybe never. I will tell you the categories of people who would have had the influence to give the staff the desired answer. There were three. There was the general counsel, who had a prior professional relationship with one of the entities involved. There were people in the governor's office whose interest in this matter was, on the face of it, not obvious but who I came to believe had been talked to. And there was at least one board member, my colleague at the time, who I think was working as an intermediary between the other two.

CM: Your colleague was being paid?

ESTHER: My colleague was not being paid. I have to be careful with you, Carlos. My colleague was, I believe, being talked to in a way that involved no money. There are arrangements in this state that involve no money. The arrangements involve the next thing that the colleague would like to be, or the appointment the colleague's spouse would like to receive, or the contract the colleague's brother-in-law would like to be in line for. None of this is illegal. None of this is even, by the standards of our state's political culture, unusual. It is the medium through which decisions are reached.

CM: That's a frustrating thing to know.

ESTHER: It is the most frustrating thing about the work. I will tell you this, and you may use it: the worst part of my last six months on the board was not that I could see what was happening. The worst part was that I could not, in any specific instance, point to a single thing that was, on its face, wrong. The wrongness was distributed across so many small, individually defensible actions that to call it wrongness made me sound like a fanatic.

That is how systems protect themselves. They distribute the wrongness so finely that no one can pick it up.

CM: You stayed for nine months after you understood. Why?

ESTHER: Because I believed, then, that there were channels I had not exhausted. I wrote to the inspector general's office. I wrote to two members of the legislature whose committees touched water issues. I wrote to a federal staffer I had known for twenty years. In each case I was told, in slightly different language, that the matter was complicated, that it was being handled, and that my intervention was either premature or unnecessary.

CM: Why did you stop?

ESTHER: Because I stopped sleeping. That was the moment. I was lying in bed in October 2021 and I was thinking, again, about the memo, and I realized I was going to have to vote on it within ninety days. And I could not bring myself to imagine voting yes. And I could not bring myself to imagine voting no and watching the vote happen anyway. So I did the thing that I had spent thirty-three years not doing. I resigned.

CM: Was it the right decision?

ESTHER: It was the decision I made. I have not asked myself if it was right. I have asked myself many other questions about it. I have not asked myself that one.

**CM notes after this session:**

The "instrumentalized" line is one of the most important things she has said. It captures, in a single word, what investigative reporting on institutional failure usually struggles to capture: that the system did not fail because anyone broke a rule, but because the rules were used in a way that produced the outcome someone wanted.

I need to find a way to use this word in the piece. Probably in chapter two. Esther's voice has to carry the line. I will ask her, before publication, if I may quote her directly.

The list of "categories of people who would have had the influence" is something I have not yet been able to corroborate independently. I have one source — Verdant Former Employee 2 — who has suggested similar things in different language. I do not have a documentary trail. The piece will, I think, need to be careful here: I can describe the dynamic in general terms, but I cannot name specific individuals as having been "talked to" without evidence that would survive legal review.

Reema will push on this. She should.
