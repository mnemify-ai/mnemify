# Interview Excerpt — Dr. Inés Maldonado-Reyes, Session 3

**Date:** 2026-03-05
**Location:** Dr. Maldonado-Reyes's office, New Mexico State University, Las Cruces
**Duration:** 2 hours 40 minutes
**Recording:** Yes (with consent)
**Transcribed by:** CM
**This excerpt:** From hour two of the session, after we had reviewed her data on the Mesa Verde watershed

---

CM: I want to ask you something that may be outside your expertise. If it is, please tell me.

DR. M-R: All right.

CM: Why did this happen?

DR. M-R: Define "this."

CM: The divergence. The paper rights detaching from the physical water. How does a regulatory system that exists to prevent exactly this kind of divergence allow it to happen?

DR. M-R: I will answer in two parts. The first part is the technical answer. The second part is the answer I am not comfortable giving, because it exceeds my expertise. You can decide what to do with each.

CM: All right.

DR. M-R: The technical answer is that the regulatory system was designed in the late nineteenth century to administer a resource that, at the time, was assumed to be roughly inexhaustible. The administrative apparatus was built to handle disputes between users who shared a watershed in real time. It was not built to handle financialization. It was not built to handle the conversion of a use right into a tradable asset detached from the place where the water flows.

When financialization happens — and it has been happening, in the western United States, for at least three decades — the regulatory system continues to function as if the underlying conditions were the conditions for which it was designed. It administers the paper. It does not administer the water. Over time, the paper and the water drift apart.

The drift is not, in any individual transaction, dramatic. The drift compounds. After several decades of compounding, the paper rights no longer correspond to the physical water in any meaningful way. The system has failed. But it has failed slowly enough that no individual administrator was confronted with the failure as a failure.

CM: That's the technical answer.

DR. M-R: That is the technical answer.

CM: What's the other answer?

DR. M-R: The other answer is that I believe — and this is my opinion, not my professional finding — that the regulatory system has, in some places, been captured. By which I mean: the staff and the political appointees and the people who have access to the staff and the political appointees are, in many cases, the same people who benefit from the failure of the system to administer water as opposed to paper. The system does not fail randomly. It fails in a direction. The direction is the direction of capital.

I would not say this in a paper. I would lose grant funding. I am saying it to you because you asked.

CM: You said you would lose grant funding. From whom?

DR. M-R: From several sources. Some federal. Some private. I do not want to get into the specifics. I have a daughter who is in graduate school. I need my income for another decade.

CM: I will not attribute this opinion to you.

DR. M-R: You may attribute it as the opinion of "an academic hydrologist who has studied the region for two decades." That is sufficiently general that I will not be the only candidate. There are four of us. The others will not say it for the same reason I will not say it on the record.

CM: All right.

DR. M-R: I will tell you one more thing. The condition you are describing is not unique to water. I have a colleague in agricultural economics who has been documenting the same pattern in farmland ownership. I have a colleague in geology who has documented it in mineral rights. The pattern is general. The financialization of natural resources, combined with regulatory systems designed for an earlier era, plus the capture of regulatory staff, equals the slow transfer of value from people who lived with the resource to people who manage capital.

This is the largest story in the western United States. Your piece will tell a small slice of it. You should be aware that the slice you are telling is a slice.

CM: I am aware.

DR. M-R: Good. Then you know what you are doing.

[End of selected excerpt. The remainder of the session covered specific data from her recent fieldwork.]

**CM notes after this session:**

The line about "the slice you are telling is a slice" is the kind of corrective every investigative reporter needs and rarely receives. Dr. Maldonado-Reyes is doing me, and the piece, a service by reminding me of the scale of the underlying problem.

The temptation, with a story like this, is to inflate it into something larger than it is. The Mesa Verde matter is significant. It is not, on its own, the only significant matter, or the largest one. The piece has to acknowledge this without diluting its own claims.

I am going to add, somewhere in the closing, a sentence or two that places the Mesa Verde story in the broader pattern. I will use Dr. Maldonado-Reyes's framing — that financialization plus regulatory mismatch plus capture equals transfer of value — but I will not attribute the opinion explicitly to her. I will write it in my own voice.

The thing I cannot write, but that I will hold in mind as I finish the piece, is the difference between this story being a story about a lake and this story being a story about a country.

Reema will, when she reads this, ask whether I am turning the piece into something larger than the evidence supports. I will tell her: not larger than the evidence supports. Larger than the original assignment. Those are different.
