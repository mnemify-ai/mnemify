# Journal — Sunday, May 17, 2026

A morning entry. Marina has gone with Beatriz to a friend's birthday party. The house is quiet. I have made coffee and I am sitting at the kitchen table, where I do not usually work, because I do not want today to be a work day. I want today to be a thinking day. The distinction may not exist for me anymore. We will see.

I have been on this piece for eighteen months. The piece is, by every objective measure, going well. The draft is at a length that Reema has authorized. The sources have, with one or two minor exceptions, agreed to be characterized in the ways the piece will characterize them. The fact-check begins in a few weeks. Publication is on the schedule the magazine wants.

What I want to write about, on this Sunday morning, is what I have noticed about myself over these eighteen months. I want to write it down because Marina is, increasingly, asking me questions about my work that I do not always know how to answer, and I want to be the kind of person who has answered the questions for himself before he tries to answer them for her.

She asked me, last week, why I write about people who have done things that hurt other people. She said: *"Doesn't it make you sad?"*

I told her: yes, sometimes.

She said: *"Why do you do it then?"*

I told her: because if I do not, then no one writes about it. And if no one writes about it, then no one knows about it. And if no one knows about it, the people who did it will keep doing it.

She thought about this. She is nine. She thinks slowly when she is thinking carefully, which I have always admired. Then she said: *"But you said the people you write about do not always know they are doing something bad. So writing about them is just telling everyone they did a bad thing."*

I said: yes, that is true.

She said: *"That seems mean."*

I have been thinking about this exchange for a week. Marina is right, in some way, about what I do for a living. The work is, in some part of it, a public characterization of people who may not themselves recognize what they have done as wrong. The work is, in another part of it, an attempt to address a public harm. The two purposes coexist. They do not perfectly align.

I do not know how to explain this to my daughter. I have not, yet, tried.

---

Beatriz has been reading drafts of the piece. She has been remarkable. She is not a journalist; she is an art historian, which means she has a different relationship to evidence than I do. She has been reading the piece as a reader, not as a fellow professional. Her readings have caught things that my professional readers — Reema, Kayla — would not catch.

The thing she said last week, after she read Chapter 5: *"Helen Fung is a great character. You are writing her well. I do not yet know what you think of her."*

I told her: neither do I.

She said: *"Then write the piece without knowing. The reader will know more than you do."*

I have been thinking about this also. It is, I think, the central craft instruction of the entire piece. I am not going to tell the reader what I think of Helen Fung. I am going to give the reader Helen Fung — her words, her decisions, her own reflections on her decisions — and the reader will decide. The decision is the reader's. Not mine.

This is, in some way, the opposite of what most contemporary investigative writing does. Most contemporary investigative writing tells the reader what to think. It does so by selection, by framing, by the cumulative effect of word choice. My piece will do all of these things in some measure. But on Helen Fung specifically, the piece will, I hope, hold itself open. The reader will close the chapter without knowing what I think of her.

If I do this right, the reader will think about Helen Fung for a long time after they finish the chapter. They will think about her, in the privacy of their own moral reasoning, in ways that will be more durable than if I had told them what to think.

That is, I now believe, what writing about morally complex people can do. The opposite of telling is what does the work. The withholding is the gift.

---

I have not been sleeping well. The piece, in its final stages, takes up the place in my consciousness that the family takes when the family is in trouble, which it is not. The piece is not in trouble either. The piece is in its proper place, which is to say in my head almost constantly.

Beatriz noticed last week. She said: *"You are at the table but you are not at the table."*

I said: I know. I am sorry.

She said: *"You do not have to be sorry. You have to come back when the piece is done."*

I will come back. I have promised myself, and her, that after the piece is published I will take six weeks. Real six weeks. No reporting. No phone calls. I will take Marina to the coast. We will read books together. I will not read anything about water.

This is the plan. I do not know yet whether I can keep it. The work has a way of finding me when I have promised not to look for it. We will see.

---

I will end this entry with a thing I want to remember.

In February, in Tucson, on the second day with Helen Fung, she said something that I did not write down at the time and that I am writing down now because I do not want to lose it.

She said, at the end of the morning conversation about her father: *"My father chose his profession because it would protect him. I chose mine because it would let me see what I was doing. He was right, for his life. I am right, I think, for mine. We arrived at the same place by different roads."*

She paused.

Then she said: *"He never had to be ashamed of his work. I have, in the last three years, been ashamed of mine. But the shame is a kind of seeing. It is, on the days I am willing to accept it, a kind of gift."*

I did not write it down in February. I am writing it down now because the line has been in my head for three months and will not leave.

The line is not in the piece. The line is, I think, between Helen and me. It is what she gave me as a person, not as a source. The piece will have other lines of hers. This one is for the journal.

I want Marina, someday, to read it. Not now. When she is older. I want her to know that this is what one of the people her father wrote about said to him, in a kitchen in Tucson, after she had decided to trust him.

I am writing this entry, in the end, for Marina to read. She will read it when she is grown. She will, by then, understand what writing is for.

She will know more about her father than her father knows now. That is, I think, what writing is for.
