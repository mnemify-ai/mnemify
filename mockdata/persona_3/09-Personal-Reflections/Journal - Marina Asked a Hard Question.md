# Journal — Marina Asked a Hard Question

**Date:** March 30, 2026

She asked at dinner. Beatriz was at the stove. I was setting the table. Marina was on the floor, drawing something I could not see.

She said, without looking up: *"Papá, ¿alguien podría hacerte daño por escribir tu historia?"*

[Papa, could someone hurt you because of writing your story?]

I set down the last of the forks. Beatriz turned from the stove. Marina was still drawing. She had not, in asking the question, looked at either of us. She was, in the way of a child asking a question she has been holding for a while, casual about it. The casualness was a way of asking without committing to the answer being important.

I sat down on the floor next to her. I asked her what she was drawing. She showed me. It was a fish.

I said: "No, mija. Nobody will hurt me."

She said: "Are you sure?"

I said: "I am sure."

She said: "But you have written about people who did bad things."

I said: "Yes."

She said: "Could they get mad?"

I said: "They could get mad. Getting mad is different from hurting someone."

She said: "How do you know they won't hurt you?"

This is the question I did not know how to answer.

What I said: "Because I have done this work for a long time and nobody has hurt me yet. And because I have a lot of people who help me, including the people at my magazine and the lawyers and your mama. And because the people I write about know that hurting a journalist would be worse for them than being written about."

She accepted this. She returned to her fish.

Beatriz, behind me, made a small sound. When Marina was not listening, in the kitchen later, Beatriz asked me: "Is that true?"

I said: "Mostly true."

She said: "How mostly?"

I said: "I am as protected as one can be doing this work in this hemisphere in this decade. I am more protected than my colleagues in Mexico whose work I respect and who have, some of them, been hurt. I am more protected than the freelance reporters in the United States who do similar work without an institution behind them."

She said: "But not completely protected."

I said: "Nobody is completely protected. The reporters who get hurt are mostly reporters in countries where the institutions do not exist. The Drift exists. I am protected by The Drift."

She said: "Until you are not."

I said: "Yes. Until I am not."

We did not talk about it more that night. Marina ate her dinner. We watched, the three of us, a few minutes of a movie before her bedtime. I read to her from a book she had picked. The book was about a girl who builds a boat.

After Marina was asleep, Beatriz and I sat in the kitchen. She made tea. We did not talk for a long time.

Then she said: "Marina has been thinking about it."

I said: "How long?"

She said: "Maybe a month. She has not asked me. She has asked her teacher one question that her teacher asked me about. She has read, on her tablet, some articles about journalists in Mexico. She has, I think, been worried."

I said: "Why didn't you tell me?"

She said: "I am telling you now."

I said: "I should have noticed."

She said: "You have been working."

I sat with this for a while. The piece I have been working on has, in some real way, been borrowing from Marina without her consent. The borrowing has been ordinary — fewer dinners, more nights when I am at the table but not at the table, the conversations with her where I am, by the standards of my own attention, half-listening. I have been telling myself that these costs are within the normal range of what serious investigative work requires of a journalist's family. I have been telling myself that the costs are temporary, that they will end when the piece is done, that the long-term return is worth the short-term debt.

The temporary nature of the costs is, however, a justification I make to myself. Marina's experience is not temporary. Marina is nine. Her experience of her father, this year, is the experience of a father who has been distracted and somewhat fearful and, in her perception, in danger of being hurt by powerful people he writes about. That experience will, whatever I do after the piece is published, become part of her relationship to me and to the work I do. It will not be undone by my taking her to the coast for six weeks in the fall.

This is, I think, the cost of the work that I have been most prepared to deny. The cost to Marina. The cost that I cannot pay in advance and cannot redistribute and cannot, in any meaningful way, compensate.

I do not know what to do with this. I have decided, in the kitchen tonight, that I am going to spend the next month being more present with her. Whatever the piece needs that I cannot give while being more present with her, the piece will not get. The piece will be slightly less good. Marina will be slightly less worried.

These are the trade-offs that the work, at this stage of my life, asks me to make. I have been, until tonight, refusing to make them clearly. I am writing this entry to commit to making them clearly from now on.

---

Marina is asleep. The house is quiet. I have washed the tea cups and turned off the kitchen light. I am writing this at the desk in my office, with the door open, which is unusual for me. I am writing it with the door open because I want, if Marina wakes up and looks for me, to be findable.

This is, I think, the smallest possible adjustment to my practice that I can make tonight. It is the adjustment I am making. The next one will follow.
