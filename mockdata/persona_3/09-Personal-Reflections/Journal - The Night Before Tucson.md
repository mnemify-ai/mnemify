# Journal — The Night Before Tucson

**Date:** February 1, 2026
**Location:** A motel room outside Nogales, Sonora

I am writing this in a motel room outside Nogales. The drive from Hermosillo today was long and the motel is bad. The sheets are clean but the light is fluorescent and the air conditioning makes a sound that I do not, even after twenty-one years of motels like this, know how to ignore.

I will see Helen Fung in the morning. I am writing this because I cannot sleep and because I have come to believe, over the years, that the night before a consequential interview is a night I should not leave unrecorded.

---

I have, in my career, interviewed perhaps three thousand people. Perhaps thirty of them have, in some lasting way, changed how I think about the work. Most of those thirty I did not recognize as consequential until later. A few I recognized immediately. Helen Fung is, I believe, going to be one of the few I recognize immediately.

I cannot explain, in advance, why I believe this. I have read her published correspondence, the legal briefs she has authored, the alumni profile she sat for in 2019. I have read between the lines of these. I have spoken with her on the phone six times. I have not yet sat across a table from her.

The thing I think is going to happen tomorrow is that I am going to be in the presence of a person who has been waiting, for three years, to say things to a stranger that she has not been able to say to anyone else. The waiting is going to come out in a particular pattern. The pattern is going to require me to listen in a way that I have not, perhaps, fully listened in many years.

I am, in other words, preparing to be the kind of journalist who is, in the moment, more useful as a listener than as an interrogator. The questions I have prepared will not, I think, be the questions that produce the most. The pauses will produce more. The silences. The willingness to wait.

This is not, by training, my default disposition. I have spent twenty-one years learning to ask the right question. I am, now, learning to ask fewer of them.

---

I called Beatriz from the road today. Marina was at school. We talked for twenty minutes about ordinary things. Marina had a math test. Beatriz had a meeting with a graduate student. The dog had eaten something he should not have eaten and was being fine but was being watched.

The ordinary things are, on a night like tonight, the things that anchor me. I have done this work for long enough that the work could, if I let it, become the whole of my life. The ordinary things at home are the reminder that there is a life outside the work, and that the life outside the work is the life the work serves.

I have, over the years, watched colleagues lose the life outside the work. The losses have been various: marriages that ended, children who grew distant, friendships that dried up. The colleagues who lost the lives have, in most cases, continued to do the work, sometimes excellently. But they have, by the time of the losses, become people for whom the work is the only thing. I do not want to become that person.

Beatriz knows this. She watches for the signs in me. She tells me, when she sees the signs, what she is seeing. I am, mostly, grateful.

Tonight I told her: "I will be home Friday."

She said: "Take Saturday with us."

I said: "Yes."

She said: "Not just at the table. With us."

I said: "Yes. I will."

I will try. I will, after Tucson, try. The piece is going to occupy me for some months yet. I want, in those months, not to be lost to it entirely. The discipline is not a journalistic discipline. It is a husband's discipline, and a father's. It is the discipline I want to be capable of.

---

In the morning I will drive the remaining hours to Tucson. I will arrive at Helen's house at 9:42. I will knock. I will not know what she will say when she opens the door.

I have, in my career, knocked on perhaps fifty doors that mattered. I have, in most cases, been received politely and have done the interview I came to do. I have, in a few cases, been turned away. I have, in two cases, been received in a way that altered the course of the piece I was working on and, in some smaller way, the course of my own thinking about the work.

I do not know which kind of reception I will have tomorrow. I am prepared for any of them. I am, more than I usually am the night before an interview, alert. Alert in a way that does not feel like adrenaline. Alert in a way that feels like the calm before something I have been preparing for, without entirely knowing it, for many years.

---

The fluorescent light is still buzzing. The air conditioning is still cycling. I will turn them both off and try to sleep. I will rise early. I will drink coffee from a paper cup at a gas station that does not yet know I am coming. I will arrive at Helen's door at 9:42, which is the time we agreed on, three minutes after she will have put the coffee on.

The coffee will, as she has warned me, be bad. I will accept it. She will offer it because she does not know any other way to receive a stranger she is preparing to trust.

This is what I have come for. Tomorrow it begins.
