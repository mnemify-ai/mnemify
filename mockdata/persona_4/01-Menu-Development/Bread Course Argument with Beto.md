# Bread Course — The Ongoing Argument with Beto

**Course:** Course 2 or no course at all
**Status:** Active disagreement
**Date:** 2026-05-26

Beto wants a bread course. I do not want a bread course. We have been having this argument for three weeks.

Beto's position: "Every tasting menu has bread. Diners expect it. They want a moment with bread and butter to settle in. It's hospitable."

My position: "Bread is what every other tasting menu does. We are not doing bread. The opener establishes the tone, course 3 lands the trout, the diner is moving. Bread breaks the rhythm and adds 40 minutes to the meal."

Yesterday Beto came back with: "Then make bread that doesn't break the rhythm."

He's annoying when he's right.

## What we're now testing

A bread "moment" that is not a course. After the opening bite and before course 3, the server brings a small board to the table:

- A 4cm slice of warm sourdough — a black-rye-and-buckwheat loaf Lila has been developing (she's better at bread than at much of her own pastry, oddly, and she's been wanting to do this loaf for months)
- A small ceramic of butter — fresh cultured butter, salted with Hokkaido sea salt and folded with a tiny amount of preserved meyer lemon zest
- A pinch of crunchy salt on the side

The diner has it for 2-3 minutes, then it's cleared. No fanfare. No "this is our bread course." Just bread.

The cost is roughly $1.40 per cover. The time impact on the meal is small if we time it right — it goes out while we're prepping course 3 in the kitchen, so the pacing actually improves.

## What Diane said

Diane is our investor and the most experienced restaurateur I know. I asked her. She said: "Bread is hospitality. Don't deny diners hospitality because you're trying to be different. Your job is to feed them well, not to make a statement about not feeding them bread."

That made me sit with it for a day. She's right and Beto is right.

## What we're going to do

Bread on the menu. Not as a course. As a hospitality moment. Lila's black-rye-buckwheat loaf with cultured butter, served warm, between the opener and course 3.

Beto is celebrating, which is annoying.

## What I'm going to call it on the menu

Nothing. The menu won't list it. The diner gets it as a small surprise. The kitchen knows about it. The servers know to time it. The diner gets a piece of warm bread and butter and they don't know to expect it and it lands as care rather than a course.

This is, I think, the right answer. Beto wanted "Bread Course" listed prominently. I wanted nothing. We landed on: real bread, no menu mention.

## On Lila's bread

Lila has been baking this loaf at home for weeks. She brings it in for family meal. The black rye and buckwheat together give it a deeply savory backbone. It's not a sweet bread, it's not a light bread. It's a serious, dense, dark loaf that tastes like the Pacific Northwest in October.

In June and July it might feel slightly heavy. Lila is working on a lighter summer version — same flour blend but with a tangzhong starter, more open crumb, slightly less dense. She'll have a version ready for tasting by next Friday.

If the lighter version is right, we use it for the opening summer menu. The autumn rotation gets the heavier original. The bread itself becomes seasonal.

## What this teaches me

I don't always know what the restaurant wants. Beto knew this one. He won this one. I'm going to remember that when he pushes back on something else.

Also: bread is hospitality. I had let my reaction against the "every tasting menu has bread" cliché override my actual job, which is to make sure the diner feels taken care of. Diane was right to call me on it.

Beto is grinning insufferably. I'm going to give him an extra day off this week to balance it out.
