# Mushroom Course — Pacific Forest

**Course:** Course 5, middle of the menu
**Status:** Locked, but rotating mushroom varietals weekly
**Date:** 2026-05-21

This is the course I am most excited about and the course that is going to be the most painful to execute consistently. The mushrooms come from Patrick at Wild Mushroom Co — he forages personally in the Coast Range and brings me whatever the forest is giving him that week. The course is built to accommodate variation. The diner gets "tonight's mushrooms" rather than a specific species. This is honest and it lets me work with what's at peak.

## The structure

- 3-4 varieties of mushroom on the plate, depending on what Patrick has
- Each treated differently — one roasted hard, one barely warmed, one pickled, one shaved raw if appropriate
- Mushroom dashi underneath (V5 dashi from the test log, the one with the local Astoria kombu) — the local kombu gives a slight mineral edge that works with mushrooms in a way Rishiri doesn't
- A small heap of crispy garlic chips
- Brown butter, frothed
- A scattering of toasted hazelnuts (Oregon, of course)
- Black pepper, crushed coarse, finishing

## What I want from this course

The diner should taste the forest. Not "mushroom flavor" — the actual specific forest where these grew. The dish lives or dies on the quality of what Patrick brings, which is why I have to keep the rest of the elements minimal. The dashi supports. The brown butter rounds. The hazelnuts and garlic give structure. Everything else moves out of the way.

## Variations I've tested

- **Chanterelle-dominant** (April): excellent. Their apricot-edge meets the dashi beautifully. Light treatment — barely warmed in butter, with just salt.
- **Lobster mushroom** (May): great in concept, harder in practice. They want hard heat. When Patrick brings them I'll pull the chanterelles back and make the lobster the focal point.
- **Morel** (April): the best version of this course I've made. Patrick brought 4 pounds in early April. We ran out in 5 days. He says we should expect another flush in June if the rains cooperate. I am praying for rain.
- **Matsutake** (autumn — testing for fall menu): the matsutake I tested were Japanese imports from Mutual Trading. Beautiful but $180/lb. For opening menu, I'm going to use chanterelles as the workhorse and feature whatever else Patrick brings as the surprise.

## The dish in practice

When a table is seated, the server tells them: "Tonight's mushrooms include [varieties]. They were foraged on [date] in [region]." This gives the diner a specific place and time. The dish becomes a postcard from a particular forest on a particular day.

If Patrick has a really exceptional flush of something specific, we'll lean into it — make the course about that single species, dial back the dashi, let the mushroom carry. This is hard to operationalize. Beto has to be ready to swap the prep on 6 hours notice.

## What I'm doing about supply consistency

Patrick is wonderful but he's one person. If he gets sick or has a bad week, the course is in trouble. Backup sources:

- Mycoterra Farm (cultivated, more reliable, less interesting)
- Patrick's apprentice Selene, who is learning his routes and can step in eventually
- Buying dried matsutake and dried porcini as ace-in-the-hole; rehydrated, they're not as good but they can save a service in a pinch

Beto has the dehydrator backup protocol written up. If we run out mid-service we can pivot to a different course concept, but I'd rather just have the right mushrooms.

## Pairing

Mateo wants a skin-contact Italian white. He's been showing me a 2021 from Radikon. It's deep, oxidative, slightly cloudy — works beautifully with the umami and the brown butter. I agreed.

Alternative pour for diners who want red: a 2020 Etna Rosso. Lighter, with the same volcanic minerality the dashi has.

## Personal note

The first time I made a version of this course was in 2017, for a one-night pop-up I did in Brooklyn. My father came up from Baltimore for that dinner. He sat at the bar and ate the mushroom course and said, "I don't know what's in this, but it tastes like the woods behind your grandmother's house."

He died two years later. This course is, in some way, for him.

I will not say that to the diners. The course is theirs. The story is mine.
