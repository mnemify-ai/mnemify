# Halibut Course Sketch

**Course:** Course 6, between mushroom and lamb
**Status:** Early sketch. May or may not survive
**Date:** 2026-05-12

I keep wanting a fish course in the middle of the menu and I keep not being satisfied with what I'm sketching. We have the trout up top (cold-smoked, light) and the lamb later (warm protein, grounded). The middle wants a different kind of fish moment — a warm flake, something that bridges the cold trout and the hot meat.

Halibut is the obvious choice but the obvious choice feels lazy. I've been resisting it for three weeks. I'm starting to think I should just commit to halibut and make it interesting.

## What halibut wants

Halibut is a fragile, lean fish. It cooks fast, dries fast, doesn't take well to hard sear. The best treatment I know is gentle poach in butter at low temperature, finish with a quick flash under salamander for color. That gives me a perfectly cooked flake that's almost custard-soft in the center.

## The plate concept

- A 60g portion of butter-poached halibut, top scored with a fine knife and dusted with a Hokkaido seven-spice (shichimi, mild)
- A small drift of charred leek — the burnt-outside, sweet-inside thing
- A spoonful of beurre blanc made with sake lees (kasu) instead of butter — yes, sake lees, it gives the sauce a fermented depth I can't get anywhere else
- A scattering of pickled rhubarb (early summer, from Persephone Farm) — tiny dice, sharp acidity, pink against the white fish
- A leaf of pickled mustard greens at the edge, like a flag

## What I'm worried about

The kasu beurre blanc. I've been making it for ten years but I've never put it on a menu because I've never trusted the line to make it consistently. Kasu is finicky — too much and the sauce gets aggressive; too little and you can't taste it. The window is narrow.

If we open with this course, Beto and I have to write the sauce protocol down precisely, train every cook on the line, and then prep the kasu base each morning so that during service it's just a finish.

This is operationally heavy for a single course's sauce. I have to decide if it's worth it.

## Backup direction

If the kasu beurre blanc is too risky, I can do a simpler version: butter-poached halibut, brown butter and capers, a piece of charred lemon, parsley oil. Bistro-style. Solid. Less interesting.

The risk is that "solid" reads as "boring" in the context of a $295 tasting menu where every other course is doing something. Halibut with brown butter and capers at the middle of a tasting menu is the kind of safe choice that makes the diner check their watch.

So either I commit to the kasu and train it relentlessly, or I find a different middle-fish concept entirely.

## Alternative middle-fish concepts I've sketched

- **Black cod (sablefish), miso-glazed.** Classic Nobu move. Too obvious.
- **Tai (Japanese sea bream).** Hard to source consistently. Beautiful when it works.
- **Local true cod, gently steamed.** Easier to source. Lower-impact flavor — almost too easy.
- **Sturgeon, smoked and warm.** Interesting. Less standard. Pacific Pearl has a steady supply.

The sturgeon is actually appealing. Smoked sturgeon, warm, with a leek-and-cream emulsion, the same pickled rhubarb. It would be a "wait, sturgeon?" moment for the diner, which is what I want.

## What I'm going to do

Test both this week. Halibut with kasu beurre blanc on Tuesday. Sturgeon with leek-cream on Thursday. Beto, Lila, Mateo, Caro, and Diane all taste both. Whichever wins the room, wins the course.

I'm pretty sure it's going to be the sturgeon. But I want to find out, not decide in advance.

## Mateo's instinct

He's pre-pairing both. He thinks the halibut wants a Riesling — Selbach-Oster spätlese. He thinks the sturgeon wants a daiginjo sake. I have not told him which way I'm leaning because I don't want him to influence the tasting.

## Personal note

This is the kind of decision that keeps me up at night. Not because it's enormous — one course is one course — but because every one of these decisions is a small bet on what the restaurant is, and they accumulate. Twenty good decisions makes a great restaurant. Twenty mediocre decisions makes a mediocre restaurant. There's no recovery from accumulated mediocrity.

So: sturgeon or halibut. Decide by Thursday.
