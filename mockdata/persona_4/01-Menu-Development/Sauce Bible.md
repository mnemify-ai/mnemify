# Sauce Bible

**Maintained by:** Aiya, with Beto
**Status:** Living document; locked at soft opening
**Date:** 2026-05-26

Every sauce on the Lark menu has a documented recipe and protocol. This is the master list. The detailed protocols are in the kitchen binder.

## Why this exists

Sauces are where restaurants live or die. A great sauce by an inconsistent kitchen becomes mediocre by service 50. The protocols are the protection against that drift.

Beto and I are writing each sauce in two formats: a "morning prep" version (large batch, made daily) and a "service finish" version (small adjustments at the pass). Each cook learns both.

## Sauces in current opening menu

### 1. Dashi (base)

Used in: trout course (the kombu wafer is brushed with reduced dashi), mushroom dashi pool, lamb glaze base, beurre blanc base.

Protocol: see Dashi Variations Test Log. Rishiri kombu, 8h cold-steep, hanakatsuo, 4min steep. Made fresh every morning.

This is the most important base in the kitchen.

### 2. Sansho oil

Used in: trout course (finishing), occasionally on the salad.

Protocol: Neutral oil (grapeseed), warm to 60°C, off heat, add freshly crushed sansho peppercorns. Steep 24h, strain. Lasts 5 days in walk-in.

The sansho has to be fresh. Old sansho oil is grassy and dull.

### 3. Kasu beurre blanc (if halibut course makes the menu)

Used in: halibut course (under decision).

Protocol: White wine reduction, shallot, sake lees (kasu), mounted with cold butter. The kasu is added during the reduction, not at the end. Pass through fine strainer.

This is the riskiest sauce on the menu. If it makes the cut, every cook on the line trains on it for a week.

### 4. Lamb glaze

Used in: lamb course.

Protocol: Roast lamb bones until deeply colored, deglaze with red wine, add dashi (Ma-konbu variant), reduce by 75%, finish with charred onion (replaced barley after V3 test). Strain, hold warm. Poured tableside.

The tableside pour is theater. Practice the carafe handling.

### 5. Beurre blanc (classic)

Used in: backup halibut, occasionally salad finishing in winter.

Protocol: Classic recipe. White wine, vinegar, shallot reduction, cold butter mounted. Pass through fine strainer, hold in bain-marie at 55°C.

Every cook in the kitchen should be able to make a perfect beurre blanc. This is fundamental.

### 6. Brown butter (finishing)

Used in: trout course (kombu wafer brush), cheese course (drizzle), mushroom course (top finishing).

Protocol: Heat butter until milk solids brown deeply, off heat just before they burn. Strain through cloth. Hold warm.

Brown butter is simple and easy to mess up. The window between properly browned and burnt is about 15 seconds at the right temperature. Time it.

### 7. Quince vinegar (if geoduck course makes the menu)

Made by me, in-house. Quinces + sake + rice vinegar mother. 8-week fermentation.

Protocol is the dish protocol — the vinegar is used in tiny amounts as a finishing acid. Not a "sauce" in the traditional sense but documented here for completeness.

### 8. Nettle purée

Used in: lamb course.

Protocol: Blanch nettles 30 seconds, ice bath, squeeze dry, blend with a small amount of spinach (for color stability), olive oil, salt. Pass through fine strainer for smoothness.

Nettle purée holds well in the walk-in for 36 hours. After that the color shifts and it goes off.

### 9. Mushroom dashi

Used in: mushroom course.

Protocol: Base dashi (V5 variant), simmered with dried shiitake and fresh mushroom trim for 1 hour. Strain.

This sauce uses what would otherwise be waste — the trim from cleaning the mushrooms for the course. Beto and I are proud of this. It tastes like the course's own deep self.

### 10. Cured egg yolk (if salad course confirms direction)

Salt-cured for 5 days, air-dried 1 day. Used microplaned over the salad course.

Stable for 3+ weeks in walk-in. Make in batches of 24, replace as used.

## Sauces I considered and rejected

- **Beurre noisette with miso.** Tested for the mushroom course. Too aggressive — the miso pushed the course toward a one-note umami. Brown butter alone wins.
- **Yuzu kosho oil.** Tested for the salad. Too strong. The cured yolk wins.
- **Ponzu.** Considered for the trout. Wrong direction for the course — the sansho oil does what ponzu would do, with less obvious Japanese reference.

## What I want cooks to learn

The order of operations in a sauce matters as much as the ingredients. The vegetables go in cold liquid for some sauces, hot liquid for others. The acid goes in early in some, late in others. Mistakes in order are not visible in the finished sauce — they only show up at the third bite, when the structure of the flavor doesn't hold.

I want every cook to understand why each step happens in the order it happens. Not just to follow the protocol but to understand it.

This is going to take months. Worth the months.
