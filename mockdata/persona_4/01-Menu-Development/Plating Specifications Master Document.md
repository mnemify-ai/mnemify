# Plating Specifications — Master Document

**Maintained by:** Aiya
**Status:** Living document; locked at soft opening
**Date:** 2026-05-26

Every course at Lark gets a plating spec. The plating spec lives in the kitchen binder. Any cook on the line should be able to plate any course identically to the spec, with no judgment calls required.

This document is the master. The individual spec sheets for each course will live in the binder.

## Why plating specs matter

Consistency is the difference between a restaurant and a chef cooking at home. The same diner returning to Lark for the third time should get the same trout course, plated the same way, every time. The first-time diner sitting at table 6 should get the same trout course as the diner at table 14.

In a 28-seat restaurant doing 5 services a week, that's 700 covers a month. Each cover plates approximately 9 courses. That's 6,300 plates a month, all of which need to look like the chef's vision.

The chef does not have time to plate 6,300 plates personally. So the plating has to be written down precisely.

## What the spec covers

For each course:

1. **Ceramic.** Which piece. Where it comes from. What the alternates are if we run out.
2. **Placement.** Where every component sits on the plate, described in spatial terms (e.g., "12 o'clock, 2cm from rim").
3. **Proportions.** Grams of each component, not estimates.
4. **Garnish timing.** What goes on at the pass, what goes on tableside (if anything).
5. **Temperature.** Plate temperature. Component temperatures.
6. **Visual reference.** A photo, taken in service lighting, of the gold-standard version. Printed and laminated, in the binder.

## Common standards across all courses

- Every plate is wiped clean by the cook plating it. No fingerprints. No drips of sauce on the rim.
- No naked olive oil pools. Oil that's poured on the plate should be in a deliberate placement, not a puddle.
- Garnish placement is final. Once a leaf or herb is placed, it does not get moved.
- Salt: finishing salt goes on at the pass, not before. Salt on a plate that's been sitting goes wet.
- Sauce: under or beside the protein, never over (with two exceptions: the lamb glaze, which is poured tableside, and the brown butter on the cheese course, which is drizzled at the pass).

## Ceramic philosophy

I have spent more time on ceramics than I expected to. Caro has been pivotal here. She has a designer's eye and has been finding pieces I would not have found.

Current ceramic sources:

- **Aaron Scott** (local potter, Portland). Most of our plates. Wood-fired, mineral glazes, slightly uneven edges. Beautiful. Expensive ($120-180 per piece) but worth it.
- **A potter in Cle Elum, WA** named Tama Sato. Smaller, simpler pieces. Used for course 2, 4, and 8. Cheaper, very functional.
- **An eBay seller** Caro found who imports vintage Japanese ceramics. We use these sparingly for special presentations. Each piece is unique. The diner doesn't know but the piece they're eating from might be 80 years old.

## Replacement policy

Plates break. They break a lot. Beto has been tracking breakage during soft openings and our rate is approximately 1.2% per service, which is normal for a tasting menu.

Aaron Scott has agreed to make us replacement pieces on a rolling basis. We pay him monthly for a batch sized to our breakage rate. This way we never run out of any specific plate.

The vintage Japanese pieces, when they break, are gone. We will gradually lose those. When we're down to half our original count, I'll stop using them in service.

## What I want the diner to feel about the plating

Cared-for. Not impressed. The diner who notices the plate too much is the diner who is not eating. The plate should feel inevitable — like of course the trout sits on this kind of ceramic, of course the radish is positioned right there.

If the diner ever thinks "wow, this is so plated," we have failed. The plating should disappear into the food.

## The brutal training piece

Beto and I are going to do plating drills with the entire kitchen team next week. Each cook plates each course five times, against the spec. We tally errors. The cook practices until they have zero errors.

This is the most boring training there is. It's also the difference between a restaurant that holds its standard at service 200 and one that drifts.

## Personal note

I learned this discipline from my chef at Single Thread. He used to come by the pass every Friday and pick one plate at random and pull it apart — the placement, the timing, the temperature. If anything was off he sent it back. I hated him for it. I miss him for it now.

I am going to be that chef at Lark. The cooks will hate me for it. They will, eventually, miss me for it when they open their own places.
