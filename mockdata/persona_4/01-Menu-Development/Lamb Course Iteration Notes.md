# Lamb Course Iteration Notes

**Course:** Probably course 7 — the protein course before dessert
**Status:** Fourth iteration. Almost there.
**Date:** 2026-05-25

The lamb is from Anderson Ranches outside Brownsville. Heritage cross — they don't tell me the breed exactly but the flavor profile is closer to a Soay than a typical American lamb, which is what I want. Grassy, slightly gamey, doesn't need apology.

Diane keeps telling me lamb is a risky choice for a Portland tasting menu. She says half the room has had a bad lamb experience. I think she's right and I'm doing it anyway. The whole point is to do it well enough that they leave saying "oh, *that's* what lamb can be."

## Current version

- Loin, cooked sous-vide at 54°C for 45 minutes, then seared in clarified butter until the fat caps
- Glaze: reduction of the lamb bones with toasted barley, dashi, and a touch of preserved Meyer lemon rind
- Charred green garlic on the plate (last of the season again — switching to charred spring onion in two weeks, same prep)
- A tablespoon of nettle purée, deep green, just spinach and nettle and a little olive oil, salt
- A single shiso leaf, deep-fried and salted
- Black sesame, freshly toasted, just a pinch

## What I'm still working

The glaze is too sweet. The dashi makes it deep but the barley reduction pushes it toward something that reads almost teriyaki, which I do not want. Going to swap the barley for charred onion next round.

The lamb is undercooked-feeling at 54°C, which is correct, but the diner who has had only over-cooked lamb their whole life is going to think it's raw. I'm not changing the temperature. I'm changing how it's presented. Sliced thinner. Three slices instead of two thick ones. So the visual reads as "this is the dish" not "this is a piece of meat."

## What Beto thinks

He thinks the shiso leaf is showing off. "It's the only Asian thing on the plate and it's sitting there like a flag." He's not wrong but he's not right either. The dish is mine and the shiso belongs.

What I might do: hide it under the lamb instead of on top. The diner gets it in the third bite and it's a surprise instead of an announcement.

Trying that on Wednesday.

## Pairing

Mateo and I have been going back and forth. He wants a pinot — there's a 2019 from Walter Scott that he's excited about. I want a more provocative pour. We landed on a 2018 cru Beaujolais from Jean Foillard, which is darker than typical Beaujolais and has the kind of earthy edge that wants game.

Compromise: we'll offer both, let the table pick. The pinot is the "safe" choice; the Beaujolais is the "trust us" choice. Whichever they pick teaches us something about the table.

## What my mother said about this dish

I haven't shown her this one. She doesn't eat much lamb. I'll wait until I'm sure.

## What I want diners to feel

I want them to take the first bite and pause. The lamb should not assault them. It should settle on the tongue and unfold. The shiso should arrive as a surprise. The nettle should ground everything. The glaze should be the bass note, not the melody.

If we get this right, this is the course people remember. Not the geoduck. Not the trout. The lamb. Because everyone has eaten lamb and they're expecting one thing, and we give them something else, and it's right.

## Risk

If we get it wrong, this is the course people remember in the bad direction. Half the room thinks it's undercooked. The other half thinks it's "too gamey." We need consistency at service level by mid-July.

Putting Beto on lamb station for soft opening. He's the most consistent cook I have. Lila as backup.
