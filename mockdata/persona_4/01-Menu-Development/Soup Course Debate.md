# Soup Course Debate

**Course:** Possibly course 2, possibly nothing
**Status:** Unresolved
**Date:** 2026-05-08

We've been going back and forth on whether to have a soup course at all. Currently the menu has the opener, then the bread moment, then trout (course 3). I keep wanting to slip a small soup in there but the rhythm doesn't quite want it.

## What a soup course would do

It would give the meal a moment of warmth in the early going. The opener (whether it's the green strawberry-fluke, the oyster-cucumber granita, or whatever we land on) is cold. The bread is warm but it's a settle-in moment, not a flavor moment. The trout is cold-smoked, so cold again.

Then we go to salad (cool), mushroom (warm), halibut/sturgeon (warm), lamb (warm). The diner doesn't get warm until course 4 or 5.

A small soup at course 2 would change the temperature arc. It would also be one more bowl on the line, one more prep, one more thing to fail at during service.

## What I sketched

A 60ml portion of consommé — a clear broth made from roasted chicken, ginger, and a slight char on the onions. Served in a small ceramic, poured at the table from a small carafe. A single chive blossom floats on top.

The broth is, technically, perfect. I've made this same consommé at every restaurant I've worked at. It's reliable, it's transporting, it's the kind of thing where the diner takes a sip and looks up.

But.

## What's stopping me

Three things.

**One:** the prep is significant. The consommé requires a 14-hour roast of chicken bones, then clarification with an egg-white raft. Beto can do this in his sleep but it's a sizeable kitchen commitment for a 60ml pour.

**Two:** the chicken is going to fight with the lamb. The umami profile of a chicken consommé and the lamb glaze (also broth-based) are too similar. The diner is going to feel them as repetition even if the dishes are radically different in execution.

**Three:** the pacing. Adding a soup course makes the menu 10 courses instead of 9. We've been timing service at the soft openings and 9 courses is already 2.5 hours. 10 courses is going to push us over 3 hours, which is too long for the kind of restaurant I want.

## What I'm leaning toward

No soup course for opening.

But: I'm going to keep the consommé recipe alive. We'll make it occasionally for staff meal so the line stays in practice with the technique. And we'll keep it in mind for the autumn rotation — when the temperature outside is colder, a warm soup early in the meal makes more sense than it does in July.

## The compromise version

Beto suggested: instead of a soup course, can we serve the lamb glaze as a small pour at the lamb course itself? A small carafe of the glaze, poured at the table, the diner watches it land on the lamb.

I love this idea. It gives the warmth-pour theater without adding a course. And it makes the lamb course more interactive.

We're testing the table-side pour next Wednesday.

## What I'm learning from this

Every course addition is a tax. The diner pays in time, the kitchen pays in prep, the dishwasher pays in ceramics. The bar for adding a course is not "is it good." The bar is "does removing it make the menu worse."

The soup course is a great course but the menu is not worse without it. So it doesn't go on.

The bread moment, by contrast, makes the meal worse if we remove it (we tested that during the second tasting, with Diane, who pointed it out: "I miss the bread"). So bread stays.

Editing a menu is exactly like editing prose. The temptation is always to add. The discipline is in cutting.

## Note to me

Stop sketching new courses for the opening menu. We have 9 courses. They are good. The work now is making them consistent, not making more of them.

This is one of the hardest pieces of advice my chef at Atomix gave me before I left: "When you open your own place, stop inventing. Execute what you have."

I have been failing this discipline for three weeks. Stopping now.
