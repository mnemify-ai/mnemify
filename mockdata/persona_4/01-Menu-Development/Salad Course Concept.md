# Salad Course Concept

**Course:** Course 4 — between trout and mushroom
**Status:** Working draft, third iteration
**Date:** 2026-05-15

This is the course that almost no tasting menu does well. The salad course is usually either an apology for not having more substantial food, or a virtuoso assemblage of 14 vegetables that the diner sees as showing off. I want neither.

What I want is a salad that earns its place. That makes the diner think "of course there's a salad here, why wouldn't there be."

## The current draft

- A composition of three lettuces from Gathering Together Farm: Little Gem (sweet, juicy), young romaine (crisp, slightly bitter), and frisée (assertive, structural)
- A bright herb scatter: tarragon, chervil, young dill, and the smallest amount of lovage
- Slivered radish — French Breakfast variety, very thin, in cold water for 10 minutes for crunch
- A dressing of cold-pressed pumpkin seed oil, white balsamic, salt
- A scattering of toasted pumpkin seeds
- Sea salt at finishing

Simple. Honest. Each component identifiable.

## What's missing

Right now the salad is good. It's not great. It tastes like a salad. Which is to say, it tastes like every salad at every nice farm-to-table place in Portland, and I'm not interested in being that.

What it needs is the one element that makes it Lark's salad. Not "I am a chef with Japanese influence" Japanese, but something genuine that I would put on the plate at home.

## What I'm trying

Three options for the unifying element:

**Option 1: Tare-glazed shiitake**
A small dice of shiitake, glazed with a tare reduction (soy, mirin, sake, kombu). Adds umami body, ties the salad to the dashi-driven savory courses. Cons: it's a strong flavor in a delicate course. Could overwhelm.

**Option 2: Cured egg yolk, microplaned**
A yolk cured in salt for 5 days, dried, then microplaned over the salad like cheese. Gives umami without overwhelming. The visual is beautiful — golden snow. Cons: requires 5 days of lead time. Operational drag.

**Option 3: A small scattering of yuzu kosho**
Yuzu kosho — Japanese chili-citrus paste, made with fermented chili and yuzu peel. Tiny dots around the plate. Strong but distinctive. Cons: yuzu kosho is a flavor most American diners don't know; could read as strange.

## My instinct

Option 2. Cured egg yolk. The 5-day lead time is fine because once cured, the yolks last 3+ weeks in the walk-in. We can have a steady supply going.

The cured yolk does the umami work the salad needs without changing what the salad is. It still tastes like a salad. But it tastes like a salad someone thought about.

## What Caro pointed out

Caro is FOH but she has good food instincts. She watched me plate version 2 yesterday and said: "The salad is the moment in the meal where the diner is going to talk. The first three courses they're paying attention. Course 4 is when they start having the conversation. Make the salad easy to eat while talking."

She's right. Whatever element I add can't require concentration to appreciate. Cured yolk passes this test — it's there as background. Yuzu kosho would fail it — every dot needs to be tasted deliberately.

## What I'm doing about herb supply

I've been worrying about herb supply for August. The herbs I want — chervil, lovage, certain tarragon varieties — are at their peak in May-June and start to suffer in late July. I've talked to three farms about extending the season through shading and timing. Gathering Together is going to set aside a section specifically for our use. We'll pay a premium.

If herbs become unreliable in August, the salad pivots to a lower-herb structure. Less interesting but workable.

## Pairing

I don't think this course needs a pairing. Pour the previous course's wine through this. Mateo agrees. The salad is a palate-cleanser as much as a course.

## What I keep telling myself

The salad course exists to do specific work in the meal: reset the palate, give the diner a moment of lightness between the warmer trout course and the deeper mushroom course. It doesn't have to be the most exciting course. It has to do its job.

Most tasting menus underestimate this course. I want to overdeliver on the work it does and underdeliver on the spectacle. Quiet competence. The salad is the course the diner remembers as "perfectly placed" even if they can't say what was on it.

That's the goal.
