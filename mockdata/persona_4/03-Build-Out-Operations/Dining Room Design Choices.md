# Dining Room Design Choices

**Status:** Mostly finalized
**Date:** 2026-05-22
**Lead:** Caro (with input from me, Mateo, Diane)

The dining room is 28 seats. That number is fixed by the kitchen's capacity to serve a tasting menu well.

## Layout

- 4 two-tops along the front window
- 1 four-top in the back corner
- A counter for 8 in front of the open pass (the chef's counter)
- 8 seats at a long communal table in the middle of the room

## Why the chef's counter

This was a fight. Diane thought we should have all booths and tables. I wanted the counter. Caro mediated.

We landed on the counter because:
- 8 seats facing the pass gives a unique experience — the diner watches the kitchen
- It changes the relationship between kitchen and dining room
- It gives me a sightline into the room from the kitchen, which I want
- It accommodates solo diners and pairs who want a more interactive experience

The cost: those 8 seats can be loud (we're worried about kitchen noise carrying), and the diners there need to be the kind of diner who wants the show.

We've designed the kitchen acoustics to manage this. We'll see in soft opening.

## The communal table

Caro pushed for this. I was skeptical. We compromised on 8 seats (smaller than she wanted; she lobbied for 10).

The communal table is for groups, but also for solo diners who'd rather not eat at the counter. The arrangement encourages conversation between adjacent diners (some will hate this; most will love it).

## Materials

- **Floor:** White oak, wide plank, oiled (not poly'd) — looks softer, ages better
- **Walls:** Plaster, with two accent walls in a deep blue-green that pulls from the moss color in the Coast Range
- **Bar (where Mateo works):** Reclaimed wood from a barn near Carlton — Diane sourced this, beautiful
- **Tables:** Walnut, custom-made by a local woodworker
- **Chairs:** Modern Danish-style, oak, fabric seats in a deep grey

## Lighting

This is the hardest part of dining room design and we have been agonizing over it.

The goal: warm, not yellow. Bright enough to see the food. Soft enough that the diner feels intimate. Capable of being adjusted from "guests arriving" mode to "deep into the meal" mode.

We landed on:
- Dimmable warm-white pendants over the tables
- Indirect lighting along the perimeter (cove lighting)
- Two spot lights at the pass (for plating visibility)
- A single dramatic pendant over the communal table

The lighting designer cost us $4,200 — which felt expensive — and was worth every dollar. The room glows in a way that I haven't seen in many Portland restaurants.

## Wallpaper for the bathrooms

Yes, we put time and money into the bathrooms.

Caro found a Japanese-style wallpaper with a delicate crane pattern. It's not loud. It's the kind of detail the diner notices in passing, registers as care, and forgets specifically but remembers as a feeling.

This is the level of detail we're after throughout the room: things the diner doesn't have to notice, but that contribute to the experience.

## What I'm worried about

- The chef's counter acoustics. Going to need real-world testing.
- The communal table. Some diners are going to dislike strangers at their table.
- The lighting transitions. We have to train the FOH team on when to dim.

## What I'm proud of

The room feels like the restaurant should feel. Caro has been pivotal here. Her aesthetic sense is exceptional and her ability to source pieces (from the bar wood to the chairs to the ceramics) has made the budget go further than it should have.

If the kitchen is mine and Beto's, the dining room is Caro's. I am letting her run it. She has earned it.
