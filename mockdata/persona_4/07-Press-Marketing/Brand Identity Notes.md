# Brand Identity Notes

**Status:** Final
**Date:** 2026-05-19
**Designer:** June Park (no relation to Diane), Portland-based

The visual and verbal identity of Lark.

## The name

Lark. Decided in November 2025 after about 30 alternatives.

Why "Lark":
- Short, memorable, says nothing fussy
- "Lark" as in the bird (small, song-driven, quietly present)
- "Lark" as in an adventure, an undertaking
- Both meanings sit well together
- No restaurant in Portland is named Lark
- Tested OK in trademark search; we registered

What I rejected:
- "Saigai" (too on-the-nose for the Japanese influence)
- "Cedar" (taken in Portland by a coffee shop)
- "Two Cranes" (too poetic)
- "Salt and Lake" (sounded like a product)
- "Tora" (means tiger in Japanese; felt cliché)
- "Hover" (too tech-startup)
- "Tanaka" (felt egotistical to use family name)
- My own name in the title (refused on principle)

Mateo found "Lark." He came home one day, said "What about Lark?" I said yes within an hour.

## The wordmark

Simple. Just the word "lark" set in lowercase letters in a slightly hand-drawn version of Goudy Old Style. The letters lean slightly toward each other, as if listening.

June (designer) drew this. We went through 14 versions before landing on this one. The version that won has slightly different proportions on the "l" and the "k" — both stretched up to make the word feel like it's reaching.

This is a $4,500 wordmark. June was patient.

## Color palette

Three primary colors:
- Charcoal (almost black, slightly warm) — #2D2A26
- Cream (warm, slightly yellow) — #F5EFE2
- Moss green (Pacific NW, alive but not bright) — #5C6E4A

One accent:
- Burnt orange (used sparingly, for emphasis only) — #BB5A2C

That's it. No additional colors. No gradients. No exceptions.

## Typography

- Wordmark: custom Goudy variant (June)
- Body: Goudy Old Style
- Display: Goudy Old Style at larger sizes
- Sans-serif (for fine print only): Söhne Buch (Klim Type Foundry)

We use one type family across everything. The discipline.

## Photography style

- Natural light
- High contrast
- Plate on a clean surface, no styling props
- Wide aspect ratio (the dishes are wide)
- Color tones that match the brand palette

We hired Jamie Wright (Portland food photographer) for the opening photo set. She got it on the first session.

## Voice

The voice across all written material:
- Direct
- Warm
- Concrete (always reach for the specific noun)
- Unpretentious
- No restaurant-speak ("an exploration of," "a celebration of," "elevated")
- Yes to: the names of suppliers, the names of dishes, the specifics of the season

Example we like:
"In June, we are using halibut from Northwest Wild Caught, chanterelles from Patrick in Estacada, and bread we baked this morning."

Example we hate:
"An exploration of Pacific Northwest bounty, elevated through classical technique and modern sensibility."

The second sentence says nothing. The first sentence tells you what dinner is.

## The website

- Single page
- Black on cream
- Short prose introduction (3 paragraphs)
- Reservation link (Tock)
- Address and hours
- Press section (linked)
- That's it

No food photos on the website. Food photos live on Instagram and in the press. The website is for diners who already know they want to come; they need to book.

This is a contrarian choice. Most restaurants have galleries. I think the gallery dilutes the meal. The diner who shows up should be surprised, not previewed.

## The exterior signage

A single carved wooden sign above the door. The wordmark "lark" in raised letters. Painted in moss green.

Carved by Marcus Wenzel, a Portland sign-carver. He took 6 weeks. The sign weighs 22 pounds.

This is a $2,800 sign. It will outlast the building.

## What I won't have

- A logo with a bird in it
- A neon sign
- Patterned wallpaper
- A merch line
- A loyalty program
- A "founder's note" on the website that's two pages long

The brand is the food and the room. The visual identity supports it. It doesn't perform it.

## Personal note

I have eaten at too many restaurants where the brand was louder than the food. June asked me at our first meeting: "What's the relationship between the brand and the experience?"

I said: "The brand should be the quiet part. The food and the room are the loud part."

She built around that. I'm grateful for her work.
