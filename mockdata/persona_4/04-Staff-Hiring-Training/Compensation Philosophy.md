# Compensation Philosophy

**Status:** Drafted; Diane briefed
**Date:** 2026-05-10

How we pay people at Lark.

## The principle

Pay above market for the role. The math is simple: turnover costs more than higher wages. A line cook who leaves after 6 months costs us at minimum $8,000 in hiring, training, and lost productivity. Paying $2/hour more keeps them.

But it's also the right thing. Restaurant workers are systematically underpaid. We can do better.

## The numbers

### Kitchen
- Line cook: $24-28/hour
- Pastry/garde manger: $25-29/hour
- Sous chef: $72-82K salary
- Executive chef: my draw, see Financial Plan

### Front of house
- Server: $22/hour plus tips (pooled)
- Host: $20/hour
- Wine director: $75K salary plus tips share
- GM: $80K salary plus tips share

### Tip pool
- 60% FOH
- 30% BOH
- 10% management (Caro and me, split)

Distributed by hours worked, not seniority.

## Benefits

- Health insurance: full coverage of basic plan, 50% of dependent coverage. Eligible after 90 days.
- PTO: 10 days for hourly, 14 days for salary, 18 days for managers
- Sick leave: 7 days, no questions asked
- Profit sharing: kicks in year 2, based on net profit
- Education budget: $1,500/year for staff stages, conferences, books

## What I won't do

- Pay the legal minimum and call it competitive
- Hire on tips alone (tipped minimum wage is bullshit; Oregon doesn't have it but I'm naming it anyway)
- Cut benefits to fund growth
- Demand unpaid trial shifts (we pay for trial shifts at full rate)

## What I asked Diane

I asked Diane to model the restaurant at higher labor cost than industry standard. She did. Labor at Lark projects to 38% of revenue, vs. the 28-32% industry standard for tasting menu places.

She said: "Higher than I'd like. Not unworkable."

I said: "It's a feature, not a bug. The food only happens if the people are taken care of."

She nodded. She gets it. She runs her own places this way (her labor is around 35%).

## The conversation I have with every hire

I tell every hire, in their first conversation with me:

"This is what we pay. This is what you'll take home. Here's the benefits. Here's when raises come. If you ever feel underpaid for what you're doing, tell me. I will explain my reasoning or fix it. I don't want anyone resenting their pay."

This is unusual. Most chefs avoid talking about money. I think it's the most important conversation we have.

## Tipping reform

I do not support eliminating tipping. I support pooled tips and good base wages. The fully-no-tips model has been tried (Bricolage, Sushi Bar, many others) and it usually doesn't work because diners don't compensate enough on the menu price.

Pooled tips with strong base wages = team incentivized to be a team, no servers making 3x the cooks, fair.

## Personal note

The first job I had after culinary school was at a place where the chef paid us in cash and yelled at us about the food cost. He drove a Porsche. I worked for him for 9 months and left $4K short of what I'd been promised.

That experience shaped me. The way I pay people is the way that chef did not pay me. Reversed exactly.
