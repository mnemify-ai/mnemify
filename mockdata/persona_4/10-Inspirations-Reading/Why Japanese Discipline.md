# Why Japanese Discipline

**Status:** Reflective
**Date:** 2026-04-30

A note on why the Japanese tradition matters to me, and why Lark is built around it without being a Japanese restaurant.

## The premise

Lark uses Japanese techniques. Dashi, salt curing, fermentation, knife work, plating composition, the discipline of mise en place. These are not the only techniques we use. They are the foundation.

We are not a Japanese restaurant. We do not serve ramen, sushi, or Japanese curry. The diner does not walk in expecting Japan.

We are a Pacific NW restaurant that uses Japanese discipline.

## Why Japanese discipline specifically

There are many disciplined cooking cultures. French. Italian. Cantonese. Korean. Levantine. All of them are deep. All of them are worthy of study.

I chose Japanese for personal and structural reasons.

### Personal
My mother is Japanese. She is from Hokkaido. The food I grew up with at home was Japanese-American — somewhere between Japanese home cooking and American 90s comfort food.

I learned the Japanese half slowly, partly from her, partly from the years in Tokyo, partly from training under Japanese chefs in the US.

The discipline is the part I took. Not the menu.

### Structural
Japanese technique is unusually well-suited to Pacific NW ingredients:
- Cold-water fish (salmon, halibut, sturgeon) responds well to Japanese fish preparation
- Mushrooms have a long Japanese tradition (matsutake especially)
- Pickling and preservation extend the season for Pacific NW produce
- Salt curing (shio-zuke) is perfect for our seasonal abundance
- Dashi is a savor base that respects ingredients rather than masking them

Japanese cuisine is built around respecting the ingredient. The cook is in service of the ingredient. The plate is composed around what the ingredient is, not what the cook wants to do to it.

This philosophy fits a restaurant whose mission is to serve Pacific NW ingredients well.

## What I do not take from Japanese cuisine

I do not take:
- The aesthetic minimalism if it means underseasoning
- The taste profile of strict Japanese food (we use more salt and more acid than a traditional Japanese restaurant)
- The pretense of authenticity (I am not Japanese; I would not claim to cook Japanese food)
- The expensive imported ingredients when local ones are better
- The structures of a Japanese menu (we don't follow a Japanese kaiseki order strictly)

## What I do take

I take:
- Mise en place discipline (taught by Japanese chefs more than any other tradition)
- The pickle pantry as a recurring source of acid and depth
- Salt curing as a technique for fish and meat
- Dashi as a flavor base
- The knife work I was trained in
- The plating principle that the plate is composed, not arranged
- The kitchen-FOH relationship structure (the kitchen serves the cook, who serves the diner)
- The expectation that the cook can be quiet and still be doing the work

## A specific technique I use every day

Salt curing.

The principle: salt the fish at the right ratio, wait a specific amount of time, rinse, dry, use.

For our smoked trout opening bite:
- 2% salt by weight of fish
- 90 minutes in the cure
- Rinse and pat dry
- Cold smoke for 12 minutes

This is a technique I learned from a chef in Tokyo. It is also a technique you would find in Pacific NW Indigenous food preparation (different salt, different smoke, similar principle).

The technique is from Japan. The fish is from Astoria. The result is Pacific NW food.

## The question I get most often

"Why Japanese influence in Pacific NW food?"

The short answer: the geographies are similar (cold water, mushroom-rich forests, marine climate). The traditions speak to each other. The discipline travels.

The long answer: my mother is from Hokkaido. Her food was the foundation of my palate. I cook from where I am from.

Both answers are true.

## What I am cautious about

- Performative use of Japanese language ("omakase," "kaiseki," etc.) at a restaurant that is not Japanese
- Claiming Japanese authenticity for techniques I have adapted
- Marketing the "Japanese-influenced" angle harder than the "Pacific NW" angle
- Treating Japanese cuisine as exotic decoration

I want the Japanese influence to be in the discipline and the technique. The diner should taste it without being told.

## Personal note

I have an uneasy relationship with the framing of "Japanese-influenced Pacific NW." Reviewers and press will love it. It is a story that travels.

But the story is reductive. I am a chef cooking what I learned to cook, which is shaped by my mother and my training. The frame is the frame, not the food.

The food is what matters. The frame can stay quiet.

— Aiya
