# Pacific NW Sense of Place

**Status:** Reflective
**Date:** 2026-04-22

What it means to me to be a Pacific NW restaurant.

## The premise

Lark is rooted here. Not "inspired by" or "drawing from." Rooted. The food cannot exist in the same form anywhere else.

This is not a marketing statement. It is the actual constraint of the restaurant.

## What "Pacific NW" means

It is a region defined by:
- The Pacific Ocean and the rivers that meet it (Columbia, Willamette)
- The Cascade Range
- The marine climate (cool, wet, mild)
- The shellfish (oysters, clams, mussels) from cold water
- The salmon and steelhead
- The white fish (halibut, cod, sole)
- The mushrooms (chanterelle, matsutake, morel, hedgehog)
- The berries (Marionberry, Olallieberry, blackberry, huckleberry)
- The stone fruit (cherries from The Dalles, peaches in Eastern OR)
- The wine country (Willamette pinot, Eola-Amity)
- The cheese makers (Cypress Grove area, Ancient Heritage in Madras)
- The dairy (Larsen, Tillamook, Nancy's)
- The grain (Bob's Red Mill and others milling Oregon wheat)
- The honey (multiple)
- The salt (Jacobsen Salt Co.)

And it is defined by the way the land here makes you cook. Wet. Cold half the year. The vegetables are deep and green. The fish is cold-water and oily. The mushrooms are dark and earthy.

## What it does not mean

- "Farm-to-table." This phrase is now meaningless. Every restaurant says it.
- A specific "Pacific NW" cuisine that existed before chefs invented it. There is no historical Pacific NW cuisine in the way there is a historical Provence cuisine. The cuisine is being invented by us, right now, over the last 20 years.
- A rejection of other traditions. The Pacific NW has been shaped by Indigenous, Asian, Black, Latin American, and European traditions for over 150 years. The food here is enriched by these, not separated from them.

## The Indigenous question

A Pacific NW restaurant has to think about Indigenous foodways. The Chinook, Klamath, Yakama, Nez Perce, and others have been here for thousands of years. Their food traditions include salmon (huge cultural significance), camas root, lamprey, huckleberry, hazelnut, lichen.

I am not Indigenous. I cook a Pacific NW menu that draws on the products of this land. The question is: do I have an obligation to engage with Indigenous foodways?

My current answer: yes, but carefully. I will not appropriate. I will not claim. I will:
- Source from Indigenous-owned producers when I find them (we have not found any yet for our scale; I am looking)
- Learn what I can about traditional Indigenous food practices
- Acknowledge that the salmon I serve was caught in waters that have been fished by Indigenous people for millennia
- Not perform any of this for press

This is a question I will revisit every year. I do not have it right yet.

## The Japanese-Pacific NW connection

Hokkaido and the Pacific NW share marine climates. The food traditions are not the same but they have natural rhymes:
- Salmon
- Cold water shellfish
- Mushrooms
- Salt curing
- Smoking
- Pickling
- Vegetables that thrive in cool wet weather

This is why a Japanese-influenced Pacific NW menu makes sense. It is not a forced fusion. The two food cultures speak to similar ecologies.

My mother understands this intuitively. She has lived in both places. She has said many times: "the food is similar; you just don't notice because you grew up in only one."

I have noticed now.

## Seasonality

Pacific NW seasons are real:
- Spring: green; everything growing; salmon coming back; first morels
- Summer: berries; stone fruit; halibut; warm-weather vegetables (briefly)
- Fall: matsutake; squash; apples; first elk
- Winter: dark greens; root vegetables; preserved foods; oysters at their best

The menu reflects this. The summer menu is light and fruit-forward. The fall menu (in progress) is heavier and mushroom-driven. The winter menu (still abstract) will be dark and warming. The spring menu (a year away) will be green.

## The Pacific NW table

A Pacific NW restaurant should feel like the region:
- Quiet
- Light gray
- Forested
- Damp
- Specific
- Not trying to be more than it is

Lark is designed to feel like this. The wood. The soft light. The fish smoke smell of the kitchen on a Thursday morning. The rain on the windows.

## What I want a diner to feel

A diner who has never been to Oregon should walk out of Lark and know what Pacific NW food is. Not because we told them. Because they tasted it.

A diner who lives in Oregon should walk out and feel they ate the place they live in. The salmon was the river they cross every day. The mushrooms were the woods they hike. The cheese was the dairy they pass on the highway.

That is the specificity I am after.

## Personal note

I am Black, Asian, American, born in Seattle, formed in NYC, settled in Portland. I am specifically Pacific NW.

The food at Lark is the food I cook because I am this specific person in this specific place. The food could not exist if I had stayed in NYC. The food could not exist if I were not the person I am.

The specificity is the point.

— Aiya
