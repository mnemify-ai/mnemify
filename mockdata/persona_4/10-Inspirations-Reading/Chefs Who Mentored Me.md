# Chefs Who Mentored Me

**Status:** Personal record
**Date:** 2026-03-28

The chefs I have learned from. Not all the chefs I have worked for. The ones who taught me something specific.

## Chef Dominique Crenn (Atelier Crenn, SF, 2014-2016)

What I learned: precision can be artistic. The plate can be a poem.

She runs Atelier Crenn with the discipline of a chef and the eye of an artist. The plates at Atelier Crenn are literal poetry — courses are titled like poems on the menu.

I did not adopt the poetry framing for Lark. But I adopted the principle: the plate is a composition.

What she said to me once: "You cook with care. Care is the harder of the two ingredients to teach. The technique you can build. The care you have."

I have carried this.

## Chef Kyle Connaughton (Single Thread, 2018-2020)

What I learned: a restaurant can be a place that integrates with a farm. The chef can think about the soil.

Kyle and his wife Katina (the farmer) built Single Thread as an integration. The farm, the inn, the restaurant. The dishes change weekly based on what Katina grew that morning.

I cannot run a farm. I run a restaurant that sources from farms. But the principle — every season, every week, build the menu around what is actually here — came from Kyle.

What he said to me: "You are an Oregon cook. You will end up there. Stop trying to be a California cook."

He was right.

## Chef Junghyun "JP" Park (Atomix, NYC, 2020-2024)

What I learned: see "Atomix Influence." Discipline, story, restraint, technique patience, supplier respect.

Plus: how to lead a team without yelling. JP never raised his voice in 4 years. The kitchen was the tightest I have worked in.

What he said to me when I left: "Open something small. Stay focused. Do not try to be Atomix. Try to be your own thing."

I am trying to be my own thing.

## Chef Aly Garcia (informal mentor, Heart Coffee Roasters, Portland)

Not a chef. A coffee roaster. But she shaped how I think about beverage programs.

What I learned: the small details of a beverage matter as much as the food. The water temperature. The pour technique. The cup.

We had coffee together once a month while I was building Lark. She tasted my coffee program and gave honest notes.

What she said: "You are running a tasting menu. The coffee at the end of the meal is the last note. Don't let it be the bad one."

## Chef Carrie Russell (Castagna, Portland, 2025)

Not a former employer but a Portland peer I leaned on heavily.

What I learned: how to navigate the Portland scene as a chef who is not from Portland. Which suppliers to trust. Which inspectors are sharp. Which contractors are reliable.

She is also a friend. We have dinner once a month.

What she said when I told her about Lark's plan: "Portland needs Lark. Portland does not know it yet. Build it."

## Chef Carme Ruscalleda (briefly, at a benefit dinner in 2017)

I cooked alongside her at a charity event in NYC. She was 65. She moved like she was 30.

What I learned: longevity is real in this work. A chef can be at the top of her game at 65 if she takes care of herself.

What she said: "Cook with your hands. Trust your hands. The hands know things the head does not."

I think about this every time I plate.

## Chef Anthony Bourdain (never met him; learned from his writing)

He is not a mentor in the literal sense. But "Kitchen Confidential" was the first book I read about being a chef. I was 16. It made me want to be a chef.

What I learned from Bourdain: be honest about the work. Don't romanticize it. The grind is real. The beauty is real. They coexist.

The fact that he died still shapes how I think about this work. The depression that visits chefs is real. The pressure is real. I am paying attention.

## My mother

She did not teach me to cook in the technical sense. She did not stand at the stove and show me how to break down a fish.

What she taught me: respect ingredients. Use everything. Do not waste. Cook for the people who will eat. Sit with them after. Notice what they leave on the plate.

She is the chef in my history I have learned the most from. She would not call herself a chef.

## What I want to do for the next generation

I want to be the chef Beto and the line cooks remember the way I remember JP. Calm. Specific. Demanding without being cruel. Generous with what I know.

If by the time Beto is 38 he is opening his own place and there is a chef on his shelf whose lessons sit at the front of his mind, and that chef is me, then I will have done this part of the work right.

That is more important to me than any review of Lark will be.

— Aiya
