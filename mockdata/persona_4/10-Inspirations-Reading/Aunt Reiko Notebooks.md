# Aunt Reiko's Notebooks

**Status:** Reading slowly
**Date:** 2026-05-26

The four notebooks my mother sent. My aunt Reiko's home recipes from Hakodate, written between 1968 and 2017.

## What the notebooks are

Aunt Reiko was a home cook in northern Japan. She kept handwritten recipe notebooks for 49 years. Each notebook has roughly 80 recipes, written in tight Japanese script with occasional Hokkaido dialect.

The notebooks are in chronological order. The four my mother sent are notebooks 2, 5, 8, and 11. Mother says these are "the ones with food you would understand."

## What I've found so far

### Notebook 2 (1972-1979)
- A recipe for nimono (simmered dish) of daikon and abura-age with a half-page note on the seasonal variation: in winter Reiko adds yuzu peel, in summer she adds shiso.
- A salt-cured trout recipe that I will adapt for the opening bite. (Different than what I am doing now but the principle — short cure, dry surface, smoke — matches.)
- Recipe for a clear soup that uses kombu and bonito 1:1, simmered 18 minutes. The standard dashi I learned at school is 20-30 minutes. Reiko's 18 minutes is the right answer, I think.

### Notebook 5 (1989-1996)
- A miso paste recipe for slow-fermented homemade miso. 18 months. Reiko notes the smell at month 6 ("strong, slightly bad, do not panic") and month 12 ("sweet, deepening"). The detail is amazing.
- Notes from the seafood market in Hakodate. Prices noted (in yen). A note about a particular fisherman who sold the best sole.
- A nabe recipe (one-pot stew) with detailed instructions about the order of ingredients. I want to do something like this at Lark in winter.

### Notebook 8 (2001-2008)
- Many family meal-style recipes. A katsudon that calls for the egg to be poured in two stages. I had not thought of that. I will try it.
- A pickled cabbage recipe that uses a small amount of kombu and konbucha powder. The umami is what makes the pickle. Genius.
- A note in the margin of one recipe: "Akiko taught me this." Akiko was my mother. I had not seen this notebook entry before. I sat with it for an hour.

### Notebook 11 (2014-2017)
- Recipes from her last years. Some are abbreviated, like she was running out of energy to write everything.
- A page that is just a list: "things I do not need to make again." Daifuku is on the list. So is karaage. So is "fancy New Year food." She was paring down.
- The last full recipe is for a simple kake-soba (hot broth and noodles) with one piece of tempura. The note: "for a quiet day."

She died 2 years after writing this.

## What I am learning from the notebooks

The home cook keeps a journal of food in a different way than the professional chef. The professional chef notes technique and yield. The home cook notes seasonality, context, family memory, and small variations.

The home cook also writes less. The recipes are shorter. The instructions assume the cook already knows. "Cook the rice well" is a complete instruction in a home notebook. In a professional kitchen we would specify temperature, water ratio, and time.

This is a kind of writing I want to learn. Confident in its omissions. Trusting the reader.

## What I will use at Lark

- The 18-minute dashi (testing it; if it works, this is a Reiko gift to the restaurant)
- The two-stage egg pour for katsudon (probably not for the menu but for family meal)
- The kombu-konbucha pickle for the cold trout course (already testing; promising)
- The principle of paring down ("things I do not need to make again")

## What I will not use

- Most of the recipes are home cooking. They are not tasting menu food. That is OK. I am not trying to extract anything for the menu. I am reading them for the philosophy.

## Personal note

I read 2 recipes a day. I am taking it slowly. By the end of summer I will have read all four notebooks.

When I am done with these, mother says she will send the others. She has 8 more.

I will read them over the next decade.

I am crying a little as I write this. I never knew Aunt Reiko well. I met her twice. The notebooks are giving me a version of her I did not have. A version that thought about food the way I think about food. A version that wrote things down so they would not be forgotten.

I will not forget her now. She is at the door of the walk-in with my mother's index card and the lamb course.

— Aiya
