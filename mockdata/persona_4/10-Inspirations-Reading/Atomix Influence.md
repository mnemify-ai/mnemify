# Atomix Influence

**Status:** Reflective
**Date:** 2026-04-15

What I took from my time at Atomix. The most formative four years of my career.

## What Atomix taught me

### Discipline
The kitchen at Atomix runs on precision. Plates leave the pass within 30 seconds of being called. Sauces are tasted before every plating. The cook who calls a course at 90 seconds is corrected immediately and kindly.

I had been in disciplined kitchens before. Atomix is different because the discipline is not enforced through fear. It is enforced through shared standard.

### Story
Every course at Atomix has a card that the diner reads. The card tells the story of the dish — the influence, the ingredient, the technique. The diner is invited into the work.

I did not adopt the card at Lark. But I adopted the principle: every dish has a reason. The reason has to be clear to the cook plating it, not just to the chef who designed it.

### Restraint
The plates at Atomix are restrained. Few elements. Each one earning its place. The diner is not overwhelmed.

I had cooked in more maximalist kitchens before. I learned at Atomix that restraint is harder than maximalism. A plate of three elements has nowhere to hide. Every element has to be exactly right.

### Patience with technique
The chef at Atomix would correct technique for hours. Knife angle. Stir direction. The lift of the spoon over the plate.

I once spent an entire afternoon learning to plate one specific course. The chef stood behind me and corrected the angle of my wrist 47 times. I was not bored. I was learning.

That patience is rare. Most chefs are too busy. The fact that he had the time and the discipline to teach me at that pace was a gift.

### Respect for sourcing
Atomix names every supplier on the menu card. The fisherman in Hokkaido. The farmer in upstate NY. The cheese maker in Vermont.

I named my suppliers because of Atomix. The diner should know who fed them.

## What I did not take from Atomix

### The cards
Atomix's printed cards are a beautiful tradition that fits Atomix. They do not fit Lark. Lark has a server who tells the story. The story is verbal, not printed.

### The minimalism of the room
Atomix is a glass kitchen surrounded by a small dining counter. Beautiful. Almost severe.

Lark is warmer. Wood. Soft light. A communal table that is not at Atomix.

### The Korean specificity
Atomix is a Korean tasting menu. Every dish is rooted in Korean food. I love it. I am not Korean. My background is Japanese-American with Pacific NW formation. Lark is the Pacific NW Japanese version of what I learned at Atomix.

### The price point
Atomix was $185 when I left. It is higher now. Lark is also $185. The food at Atomix is more elaborate per plate than Lark will be. The diner gets a different experience at the same price point. That is fair. We are not Atomix.

## A specific memory

Late November 2022. A rainy night in NYC. I was on the cold appetizer station. I was tired. I had a bad week — relationship issues, the city wearing on me, missing the West Coast.

Chef came to my station after pre-service. He looked at my mise en place. He looked at me. He said: "your station is clean and your plating yesterday was good. Cook for the diners tonight. Forget everything else for the next 5 hours."

I did. I had the best service of my time there. He noticed and acknowledged after.

That was the moment I understood what a kitchen chef does. He is not a yelling person. He is a person who knows what each cook needs to hear that night. Sometimes "your station is clean." Sometimes harder things.

I want to be that for my team.

## What I am still learning from Atomix

Even now I think about specific dishes I ate there. The taste of the year-old kimchi consommé. The texture of the abalone porridge. The smell of the room when the kitchen was making the shoyu reduction.

These tastes shape what I cook. I do not copy them. I am informed by them.

## Personal note

The chef at Atomix gave me a wooden spatula as a parting gift when I left. He had been using it for 7 years. He had carved the handle to fit his hand.

The spatula sits on the prep counter at Lark. I use it every day. The handle does not fit my hand exactly. I am wearing it into the shape of my hand over time. By the end of year 1 at Lark it will fit me.

The fact that I am here is partly because of him.

— Aiya
