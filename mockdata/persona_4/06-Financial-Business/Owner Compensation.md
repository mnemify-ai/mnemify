# Owner Compensation

**Status:** Set with Diane
**Date:** 2026-05-08

What I pay myself. This is uncomfortable to write but I think it's important to be clear about.

## Year 1

- Salary: $65,000
- No bonus
- No profit share until year 2
- Full meal during shift
- Health insurance covered

## Why this number

It's below market for a chef of my level (an executive chef at a comparable Portland restaurant earns $85-110K). It's deliberately low because:

1. Lark is in its first year; the business needs cash
2. I have a personal guarantee on the lease; the business needs to be financially strong to relieve me of that
3. I would rather pay the team well than pay myself well in year 1
4. I have savings to draw on if needed (~$35K)

Diane and I went back and forth. She wanted me at $85K. I refused. We settled at $65K with the agreement that:
- Year 2: $80K if Lark hits projections
- Year 3: $95K if Lark is steady
- Profit share kicks in year 2

## Mateo's compensation

Mateo is $75K as wine director. He's salaried, he's senior, his role is more clearly comparable to a market rate.

This is more than I pay myself. I am fine with this. He earned it; he is the wine director; he is also putting in 70-hour weeks.

This is a feature of our partnership. We are partners in life and in work but not interchangeable in role. Mateo's role is more clearly defined and he should be paid for that role accordingly.

## What I am sacrificing in year 1

The financial reality of $65K in Portland with our cost of living:
- Mateo and I share housing ($2,800/month)
- We are not buying anything substantial
- We are not saving in year 1
- We are dipping into savings to cover the gap
- We are postponing personal financial goals

This is OK because:
- It's year 1
- We chose this
- The upside is real if Lark works

## What I will not do

- Pay myself a bonus that the business can't justify
- Take a distribution that depletes the operating cash reserve
- Hide owner-perks (no personal expenses through the business)
- Pay myself before payroll if cash is tight (this is the rule Diane drilled into me)

The principle: the business is not me. The business has its own balance sheet. I am the chef and an owner. Those are different things.

## What I want by year 5

- $130K salary
- Profit share of 8% of net income
- Equity worth real money (modeling Lark at $2M valuation by year 5)
- Some kind of secondary income source (cookbook, consulting, the Option B concept)

This would be a real living. It is also probably realistic.

## What about my mother

My mother has been retired since 2018. She has savings and Social Security; she is not dependent on me financially. But I expect to be supporting her more as she ages. Year 4 of Lark is when she's 72 and probably needs help with the house in Seattle.

I am planning for this. My financial trajectory accommodates it.

## What I have not figured out

- Health care decisions if my mother needs assisted living
- Whether to buy a house in Portland or keep renting
- Whether I want kids (I am 38; this is a real conversation Mateo and I are having)

These are not Lark questions. They are life questions that intersect with Lark.

## What Diane said

"Pay yourself enough that you're not bitter. Pay yourself little enough that you respect the business."

I took this to heart. $65K in year 1 is the line.

## Personal note

A friend of mine who runs a restaurant in Brooklyn pays himself $200K and his restaurant is on its second round of debt restructuring. Another friend in Chicago pays himself $40K and her restaurant is debt-free and growing.

The pattern: how you pay yourself in the first three years matters more than people think. I am choosing to start low and grow into market rate as the business earns it.

I want Lark to be debt-free at year 5. The way to get there is to pay myself less now and let the business build a balance sheet.

That's the bet.
