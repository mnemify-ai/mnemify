# 2026-05-10 — On my mother

Mother's Day. Spent the morning on the phone with my mother in Seattle.

She is 70. She is fine. She is also smaller every time I see her. The summer I will see her at opening (she's flying down on July 11; staying through the 14th) she will be smaller than the last time I saw her.

This is the truth of having a parent at 70. They are still themselves and they are also moving toward a different version.

She asked about the restaurant. I gave her the version I always give: it's hard, it's exciting, the team is good. She asked about Beto. She asked about Mateo. She asked if I am sleeping.

I lied. I said I am sleeping. She knew I lied.

Then she said something I want to write down. She said:

"When I opened the grocery in 1985 I did not sleep for two years. I slept after. You will sleep after. You are doing the right work. Do not lose yourself."

This is the longest piece of advice she has ever given me about my career. My mother is not a piece-of-advice person. She is a I-will-show-you-by-living person.

The "do not lose yourself" was the operative phrase. I am chewing on it.

What does it mean to lose myself in this restaurant?

I think it means: cooking for what I think the diner wants instead of cooking for what I think is true. Designing for Instagram. Saying yes to opportunities that fragment the work. Letting the team be a team I would not want to be a part of. Living for the restaurant only.

I do not think I am doing those things yet.

But the path to them is short and the slope is slippery. Restaurants do this to chefs. The chef starts at a vision and within a year is performing the vision instead of living it.

My mother is telling me to live the work. Not perform it.

I am going to tape that note (the phrase, on a small index card) inside the door of the walk-in. So when I open the walk-in to start prep every day I see it.

"Do not lose yourself."

She also told me she is sending a box of cookbooks. The cookbooks of her older sister, my aunt Reiko, who died in 2019. Aunt Reiko was a home cook in Hakodate. She wrote down every recipe in notebooks. My mother has 12 notebooks. She is sending me 4.

I am crying as I write this. The notebooks will be the most important reading I do this year.

Aunt Reiko once made me a meal in Hakodate when I was 19. Cold tofu with a soy and ginger sauce, miso soup with three kinds of mushrooms, salt-grilled mackerel, white rice, pickled cucumber. I have remembered that meal for 19 years. It was perfect.

The notebooks will arrive this week. I will not read them all at once. I will read them slowly.

Mother's Day. I love my mother. She is my reason.

— Aiya
