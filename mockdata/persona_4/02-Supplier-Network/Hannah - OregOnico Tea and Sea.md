# Hannah — OregOnico Tea and Sea

**Relationship:** Specialty teas, local seaweeds, sea salt
**Located:** Astoria, OR (coast)
**Contact:** Hannah herself
**First met:** 2025-08 at a foraging workshop
**Delivery cadence:** Monthly, sometimes more for tea
**Status:** Special-application supplier

## Background

Hannah is a one-person operation. She forages seaweeds along the Oregon coast and processes them into dried products. She also makes a small line of teas (mostly herb-and-seaweed blends, no actual tea leaves) and sells locally-harvested sea salt.

Her main product line for me is dried seaweeds. The kombu-style she harvests near Astoria is interesting — different from Rishiri but distinctive.

## What I order

- **Local kombu-style seaweed** (used for the mushroom course dashi — V5 variant)
- **Dulse** for occasional use (savory garnish)
- **Sea salt** (for table service, finishing)
- **Specialty seaweed mix** for staff meal occasional use

Monthly spend roughly $250-400.

## Pricing

- Local kombu: $42/lb (expensive but small quantities needed)
- Dulse: $28/lb
- Sea salt: $18/lb

## How we work

Hannah and I communicate sporadically. She tells me what's ready and at what quantity. I order what I want.

She makes deliveries to Portland once or twice a month, combining with other chef customers' orders to make the drive worth it.

## What worries me

Hannah is one person. If she stops or scales back, I have no equivalent supplier. The local kombu specifically is unique — I can substitute Rishiri but I lose the regional specificity.

Backup: rely on Rishiri exclusively, lose the V5 dashi variant.

## What Hannah told me on my visit to Astoria

We were walking on a beach near Cannon Beach. She pointed at the rocks and said, "Most of this seaweed is edible. Most chefs don't know that."

I had been a chef for 15 years and I had not really known this. I started learning then.

## Personal note

Hannah is the kind of supplier who is more an artist than a vendor. She does this work because she loves the coast. The economics don't entirely make sense — she could earn more doing something else.

I make sure to pay her quickly and to acknowledge her work publicly when it makes sense (mentioning her by name to journalists when they ask about sourcing, for example).

## What I want long-term

Hannah keeps doing this work. Lark continues to be a stable customer. The local kombu becomes a known feature of our dashi.

If she eventually wants to scale up or train an apprentice, I'll help where I can.

If she decides to stop, I'll respect the decision and adapt the menu. The work is hers; the work she's doing isn't a permanent guarantee to my restaurant.
