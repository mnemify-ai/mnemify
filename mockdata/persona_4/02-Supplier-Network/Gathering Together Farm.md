# Gathering Together Farm

**Relationship:** Vegetables, herbs, salad greens — backbone of the produce program
**Located:** Philomath, OR (just outside Corvallis)
**First met:** 2025-08, on a farm visit Diane organized
**Delivery cadence:** 3x weekly
**Status:** The most important supplier on the menu

## Background

Gathering Together Farm is one of the most respected mid-size organic farms in Oregon. They have been around for 35+ years. They supply restaurants up and down the I-5 corridor and also run a weekly CSA and a farm stand.

John Eveland (one of the founders) is still active. His daughters Rosa and Anna run most of the operations now.

## What they bring

Almost everything green and rooted on my menu:

- All salad greens (Little Gem, romaine, frisée, arugula, tatsoi)
- All herbs (tarragon, chervil, dill, lovage, parsley, sorrel)
- Roots (radishes including black radish, baby turnips, sunchokes)
- Alliums (green garlic in spring, spring onions, leeks, scallions)
- Brassicas (when in season)
- Tomatoes in summer
- Stone fruit through their orchard partnerships

For Lark specifically, they're growing a custom selection of herbs in a section they call "the chef block" — varieties I requested, planted for the restaurant's specific use.

## The economics

We have a flat seasonal commitment plus order-on-demand. I commit to roughly $4,500/week in average produce spend during peak season, which gives me priority routing and price stability. They charge slightly below market rate as a result.

Specific items are itemized:
- Salad mix: $8/lb
- Herbs: $14-22/lb depending on rarity
- Black radish: $4/lb (rare)
- Tomatoes: $4-6/lb in season

## How we work together

Rosa sends me a weekly "availability sheet" every Sunday. I respond Monday morning with my order for the week. They deliver Tue/Thu/Sat mornings before service prep.

I visit the farm every 4-6 weeks. Not for any formal reason. I just like being there.

## The herb block

I want to talk about this because it's the most distinctive thing about our relationship.

In February I sent Rosa a list of 14 herbs I wanted access to that aren't standard for Pacific Northwest production: French tarragon (not Russian), real chervil (most "chervil" you can buy is overgrown), lovage, sorrel varieties, certain shiso cultivars, and a few others.

She and her team set aside a 1/4-acre section and planted them. They're harvesting from this block specifically for me. The herbs are noticeably better than what I'd get otherwise.

The cost of this arrangement: $800/month, paid as a herb-block retainer in addition to per-item pricing. I think it's a steal.

## What worries me

The farm is wonderful and stable but they are extremely busy. They prioritize customers who are good citizens — pay on time, don't haggle, don't make last-minute changes. I aim to be that customer.

If a particular season's weather is bad, the herb block could be affected. We have a verbal understanding that they'll communicate immediately and we'll adapt.

## What Rosa told me on my first farm visit

We were walking through a field of arugula. She said, "Chefs who do well with us are the ones who plan a season ahead. Chefs who struggle are the ones who only think a week ahead."

I am trying to be a chef who plans a season ahead. The autumn menu sketches I've been doing are partly for this reason — Rosa wants to know in May what I want them planting for September.

## Personal note

Last fall they sent me a box of late-season heirloom tomatoes "as a gift." There was a note: "We saved these for you because the chef who tasted them last year cried."

I had cried, the year before, at a tasting where they served their late-season Brandywines with just salt. I had been having a bad week. The tomatoes were so good I couldn't help it.

Rosa remembered. That's the kind of supplier they are.

## What I want long-term

Gathering Together is our farm. The relationship deepens. When I open my second restaurant (someday, maybe), they're the produce supplier there too.

If the herb block experiment works through 2026, we expand to a 1/2-acre commitment for 2027. Eventually it becomes a permanent feature of their farm's operations — Lark herbs, the way some farms have a chef block dedicated to a single restaurant.

This is what I want. This is the kind of long horizon that produces good food.
