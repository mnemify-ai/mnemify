# Anderson Ranches — Lamb

**Relationship:** Lamb producer, primary supplier for lamb course
**Located:** Brownsville, OR (southwest of Eugene)
**First met:** 2025-11 at a chef-rancher dinner in Salem
**Delivery cadence:** Every two weeks; I order ahead based on covers
**Status:** Critical to the lamb course

## Background

Anderson Ranches is a fourth-generation family operation. Reed Anderson runs it now; his father Mike still helps with the breeding decisions. They raise a heritage cross they developed themselves — Reed won't tell me the exact crosses but it's some combination of Katahdin, Soay, and what he calls "the old sheep my grandfather had."

The flavor profile is distinct. American lamb is usually mild-to-bland. Anderson's lamb has the gaminess of European lamb without the muttoniness. It's what I want.

## How we work together

I send Reed a forecast every two weeks: expected covers, expected portion size, expected cuts. He pulls from his harvest schedule accordingly.

I take primarily the loin, with some leg and shank cuts for staff meal. The trim goes into the lamb glaze (the bones are the glaze base).

He charges $14.50/lb on the loin, $9/lb on the leg, $6.50/lb on the shank. This is fair-to-high. Worth it.

## The harvest cycle

Anderson harvests on a 4-day rotation, with USDA inspection at their farm. The harvest dates matter to me because aging is critical. I want lamb that has aged 7-14 days from harvest.

- Day 0: harvest
- Day 7: ideal for braising cuts (shank, shoulder)
- Day 10-14: ideal for loin (allows enzymatic breakdown for tenderness)

Reed knows my preferences. He sends me lamb that's been aged appropriately for what I asked for.

## What I love about working with Reed

He calls me when he has a particularly good animal. Last month he sent me three loins from a single ewe that he said was the best he'd raised in three years. The meat was extraordinary. He charged me normal price because that's the deal — when good ones come, they come.

He also tells me when an animal is not great. He doesn't pretend. He says: "This one was fine but not special. I'm sending it at standard price; let me know if you have any issues."

This honesty is what makes the long relationship possible.

## What worries me

The lamb supply nationally has been getting weirder. Demand is up. Some California restaurants are paying premium prices for Anderson's lamb. Reed has told me he won't drop me for higher-paying customers but I worry about pricing pressure over time.

I'm budgeting for a 15% price increase by 2027.

## What Reed told me on my first visit to the ranch

He took me out to one of the pastures in November. The sheep were grazing in a field that had been seeded with native grasses. He pointed and said, "This is the work. The lamb is the consequence."

I think about that a lot. Most restaurants talk about ingredients as if the ingredient is the work. Reed's framing is that the work is the land, and the meat is what comes out of the land being treated right.

## Personal note

I went to Reed and his wife Lisa's house for dinner once in February. Lisa made a leg of lamb from one of their animals, with rosemary and garlic, the way her grandmother taught her. It was the best lamb I've ever eaten and there was nothing technique-y about it. The animal was the right animal. The cooking was respect.

I keep that meal in my head. It's a reminder that the meal isn't about my technique. It's about the animal, treated right, by the people who raise it and the people who cook it.

## What I want long-term

Anderson stays our primary lamb supplier through Reed's career. When Reed retires (he's 52, so this is 15+ years out), the ranch transitions to either his nephew or sale to a similar operation. We continue the relationship with whoever inherits.

The lamb course at Lark, in 10 years, still comes from Anderson Ranches. That's the dream.
