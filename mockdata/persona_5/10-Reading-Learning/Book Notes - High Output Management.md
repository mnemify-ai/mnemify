# Book Notes — High Output Management

**Book:** *High Output Management* by Andy Grove
**Read:** April 2026 (re-read)
**Rating:** 9/10 — still the best engineering management book ever written

## Why I re-read this

I've been struggling with the "how do I scale myself" question. The therapist conversations have circled it. Old wisdom from Grove felt like the right place to go.

Last read this in 2019 when I was a senior IC at Plaid. Different reader now.

## What hit different this time

### The output of a manager is the output of their organization, not their own work

This is the single sentence I should have on a sticky note. I keep slipping into "but I personally shipped X." That's IC thinking. The job is now the team's output.

The April incident: my personal heroics in handling it well don't matter as much as whether the team had been set up to prevent it. They weren't. That's the failure to absorb.

### One-on-ones are the highest leverage activity

Grove says 1:1s should be ~90 minutes, weekly with each direct report.

I run 30-minute 1:1s. That's wrong. I'm doing the management version of "I'll get to the deep work later" — never. The 30-minute slot enforces shallow conversation.

Going to try 60-minute 1:1s with each TL starting next week. See how it goes. 90 might still be too much for our cadence.

### Decisions are made best at the lowest level possible

I make too many decisions. Some of them should be made by Marcus, Nadia, or Devon without my involvement. I tell myself I'm "being available" when really I'm bottlenecking.

Specific test: at the end of each week, list every decision I made. For each one, ask: should this have been made one level below?

My guess: 30% of my decisions should be made one level down.

### Task-relevant maturity matters

Grove's framework: how directive your management style should be depends on the employee's task-relevant maturity, not their general experience.

Marcus is high-maturity on backend architecture (don't manage closely), medium-maturity on hiring (more involvement helpful), low-maturity on cross-team coordination (more directive).

I treat Marcus uniformly. Wrong. Different maturity per area requires different management style per area.

### Meetings have specific types and purposes

Grove distinguishes:
- Process-oriented meetings (1:1s, staff meetings, operations reviews)
- Mission-oriented meetings (decision-making, problem-solving, planning)

The former should be regular and recurring. The latter should be ad-hoc and triggered by need.

I have too much of the latter dressed up as the former. The weekly "tech-product sync" we just killed is a perfect example.

### Indicators that lead vs. indicators that lag

The output metrics I track (MAU, ARR, deployment frequency) are lagging indicators. The leading indicators — engineer satisfaction, time to first commit for new hires, PR cycle time — I don't track formally.

Grove's framing: management is about steering, and steering requires leading indicators. Lagging indicators tell you the past.

Going to identify three leading indicators for my engineering org and track them quarterly. Candidates: PR cycle time, time to ship from spec, on-call page frequency.

## Where Grove is dated

The book is from 1983. Some of it shows:
- The world of "all your employees are in one building" doesn't match modern hybrid
- Some of the productivity examples are factory-floor specific
- The hiring discussion predates today's market dynamics
- AI assistance to engineers doesn't appear

But the management theory holds. The technology has changed; the human dynamics haven't.

## What I'm actually going to do differently

1. **Lengthen TL 1:1s to 60 minutes.** Try for a month, evaluate.
2. **Track decisions I make weekly.** Honest review.
3. **Define three leading indicators for engineering health.** Start tracking quarterly.
4. **Stop treating direct reports uniformly.** Adjust style per area per person.
5. **Stop doing IC work that delegating would solve.** Make the list from therapy concrete.

## What I'm not changing

- The flat-ish org. We're too small for thicker hierarchy. Add the VPE; otherwise stay flat.
- The async-first culture. Working great.
- The light-process approach. We've earned that and it's working.

## The most useful re-frame

"My output is what my org produces" — and so when I'm tempted to write a piece of code myself, I should ask: would me writing this code add more value than coaching an engineer to write it well?

Sometimes the answer is yes (urgent, sensitive, area I uniquely know). Mostly the answer is no.

## Verdict

Best management book. Read it again next year. Probably again the year after.
